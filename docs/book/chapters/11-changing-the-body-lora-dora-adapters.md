# Chapter 11 - Changing the Body: What a LoRA Actually Is (and DoRA, and the Family)

Every instrument can be restrung, refretted, re-bodied. The question is never whether you *can* change the wood — it is what the change costs, how long it lasts, and how much of the old character walks out with the sawdust. A luthier who replaces a top plate gains a new voice and loses the one the player had grown into; a luthier who slips a thin brace under an existing top changes the response without unmaking the instrument. Both are real interventions, not the same kind of act, and confusing them is how you ruin a good guitar.

This chapter is the book's first encounter with the most consequential intervention site of all: not the words going in, not the latent mid-carve, but **the weights themselves** — the billions of learned numbers inside the generative model that encode everything it knows about sound and about how a phrase like "warm dub bassline" should bend the carving. Every dial we have touched so far (the prompt, guidance, re-melting, masks) left the body untouched and worked at performance time. Here we reach inside and change the substance — and we change it *before the performance begins*. The sigma walk of Chapter 4 has not yet started when a weight-change takes effect; a mask freezes material mid-walk, a control head reaches in while sigma descends, but a LoRA does neither. It is fixed before the first grain of noise is laid down: whatever it teaches, it teaches the carving tool, not the cut.

There are two philosophies for doing it. One rebuilds the body; the other bonds a removable laminate to it. Understanding why those differ in kind, not merely in degree, is the whole work of this chapter.

## Rebuilding the Body: Full Finetuning

The most direct way to change what the instrument does is to keep teaching it. Gather a corpus in the sound world you want and let the model keep learning from it, exactly as it first learned, except that now every weight is free to drift in response. Feed it enough Bavarian brass band recordings and the model's tendencies shift toward them: it reaches, by default, for those timbres, those cadences, those idiomatic turns. This is **full finetuning**, and it genuinely moves the body of the instrument.

It is also the most expensive and least reversible thing in this book. You are re-graduating the entire soundboard — thinning here, stiffening there — across its whole surface at once, and two costs follow. The first is sheer effort: full finetuning of a model this size is not a weekend job, but tens to hundreds of hours of curated audio and serious accelerator time, because no small corner of the model is held still.

The second is subtler and more dangerous: **the instrument forgets.** Push the weights hard toward your brass band and the model's grip on what it used to do well can quietly erode — and it erodes in a telling order, the capabilities *least represented in your new corpus* going first. Finetune on hours of dark ambient drones and weeks later the model has lost its reliable bright transient detail: ask for a crisp hi-hat or a snapped pluck and it reaches, sheepishly, for a soft wash, because nothing in its recent schooling reminded it that audio can be fast and bright. The edges of the old range fade before the centre. The technical name is catastrophic forgetting, but the luthier already knows it: you cannot aggressively re-voice one register without risking the others, because it is all one piece of wood. And a finetune is more or less permanent — you can finetune again, but you cannot peel the change back off. The old instrument is gone.

Finetuning has its place — when you truly want a different instrument and have the data and patience for it. But for most of what a musician-builder wants to try, rebuilding the body is far more surgery than the goal requires.

## The Laminate: What a LoRA Actually Is

LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation — begins from a refusal: it does not touch the existing weights at all. Freeze them; let the instrument keep every fibre of its body as built. Then, *alongside* certain chosen internal matrices, slip in a small, separate correction that rides on top of what the frozen weight already does.

Picture a thin, precisely shaped laminate bonded to the soundboard. The plate is untouched beneath it; the laminate biases its response in a direction you have chosen — warmer, brighter, quicker to speak — and because it is a separate piece, you can shape it, dial how firmly it presses, or lift it off entirely. The body keeps its character; the laminate adds a lean. Hold that image; the rest of the chapter leans on it without re-drawing it.

The defining trick is in the word "low-rank," worth feeling rather than calculating. The correction is deliberately forced through a **bottleneck** — squeezed to a narrow channel and expanded back out — so it has only a handful of independent degrees of freedom rather than the sprawling freedom of the matrix beside it. That constraint is the whole point. A correction with few knobs can be learned from little data — minutes to a couple of hours of audio is often plenty, where the full finetune wanted tens or hundreds — stored in a tiny file, and, crucially, it tends to learn a *direction of adjustment* rather than memorising a thousand unrelated details. You bond a laminate with a definite grain; you do not re-carve the plate freehand.

Where does the laminate stop being enough? Below a few hours of data, LoRA is the only option; above tens of hours either is viable and the choice is permanence versus reversibility. But the deeper boundary is not data, it is *depth of effect*: a thin shim can lean the existing world, not supply a new one. When the change you want is so pervasive it must touch every corner — a different instrument, not a biased one — you stop shimming and rebuild the body.

Where does the laminate bond? Most often at the same internal junctions we met in Chapter 5 — the attention layers, including the cross-attention door where the prompt's authority enters. We return to this below: *where* you attach the correction shapes *what* it can teach.

## The Training-Time Change That Behaves Like a Runtime Control

Here is the quiet magic. The learning happens once, at training time — but because the correction is a *separate, additive* piece never baked into the frozen body, what you are left with behaves at performance time like a set of live controls. The finetune gives you a new instrument and nothing more; the laminate gives you a new instrument *and a handful of knobs over how much of it you are using* — and all four turn during generation with no further training:

- **Dial its strength.** Full force, a whisper, or anywhere between. A style learned at full intensity can be applied at a quarter for a subtler inflection. The training was fixed; the dose is not.
- **Stack laminates.** Bond a rhythmic-feel correction, a timbral one, and a production aesthetic all at once. They combine *at the moment you press play* — you assemble the voice live, from a shelf, rather than in some prior run that fused them.
- **Blend.** Mix two corrections by weight, so the space *between* two laminates becomes a continuous dial you sweep in performance. Finetuning, having baked everything into one inseparable body, cannot offer this at all.
- **Peel it off.** Detach the laminate and the instrument is, to the number, exactly what it was before. Nothing overwritten, nothing forgotten.

So a LoRA is a *training-time* change to the weights that hands you, in return, a fistful of *inference-time* dials. This is the first coordinate system in the map this book is building (Chapter 14 gives every method its formal address): finetuning sits at *training-time, holistic, non-reversible*; LoRA sits at *training-time, surgical, reversible, stackable*. Same wall (the weights), same moment (before the carving), opposite corners on permanence. Feel that one difference and the rest of the map's axes come easily.

## When the Laminates Fight

Stacking is the most seductive promise, so it deserves an honest warning before you build your shelf. A laminate biases the plate, and two biases on the same patch of wood do not politely average. Two LoRAs that both lean on the low end add their pressures where they overlap, and the stack can boom or smear where neither did alone; two that colour the *same* attention junction in different directions compete for the same handful of degrees of freedom, and the louder wins in a way you did not ask for. The corrections that stack most gracefully tend to touch *different* things — a rhythmic-feel laminate and a timbral one are likelier to coexist than two timbral ones — but even that is a tendency, not a law.

This is the first wall you hit when composing interventions, and not the last: the same caution returns in Chapter 13, the moment you stack a LoRA *and* a control head *and* guidance in one run. Three pressures on one plate, two set-and-forget and one correcting in real time — do they cooperate or interfere? We genuinely do not know in general, and that is a frontier worth curiosity rather than trust. Stack deliberately, listen hard, add one thing at a time.

## How You Know It Worked

Which raises the question every builder asks too late: how do you *know* a laminate did what you wanted, rather than something you talked yourself into hearing? A LoRA almost always changes *something*, and the ear is generous to its own effort, so the danger is mistaking *any* change for the *intended* one. Three habits guard against it. Generate the same prompt *and the same seed* with the laminate off and on, and listen to the pair — fixing both isolates the laminate's contribution from the ordinary variation between takes. Sweep the strength knob up from zero and check that the quality you care about moves *monotonically and in the direction you trained*: if "warmer" arrives at a quarter, vanishes at half, and returns inverted at full, you taught noise that happens to sound warm at one setting, not warmth. And test on prompts *outside* the training material; a laminate that only steers the phrases it was trained on has memorised, not generalised. The discipline rhymes with the one Chapter 13 builds for control heads, but the laminate needs its own version, because it has no readout, no number to watch — only your ears and the honesty of a clean A/B.

## DoRA: Separating Tension from Voicing

DoRA refines the laminate idea by noticing something plain LoRA blurs together. Any correction to a weight has two independent qualities: *how much* the response shifts and *which way* it shifts. Plain LoRA learns both tangled into one bundle; DoRA pulls them apart, a separate handle for magnitude and one for direction.

The luthier already lives inside this distinction. A string has a **tension** — how tightly it is wound, which sets how forcefully it speaks — and a **voicing** — the timbral colour of how it speaks, shaped by where and how it is excited, by the material, by the bridge. Coupled in feel, separable in fact: you can tighten a string without changing its essential timbre, and re-colour its voice without changing its pitch. A builder who can adjust the two *independently* has finer command than one who can only twist a peg and accept whatever timbral side-effect comes with it.

That independence is what DoRA buys, and it matters because real full finetuning, watched closely, mostly changes *direction* while keeping *magnitude* on a shorter leash — so DoRA mirrors the natural motion and learns more steadily from the same small dataset at much the same cost. Which musical jobs benefit most? The ones about *re-colouring without re-forcing*: a new timbral tint, a new vowel at the same loudness, a shift in *character* that must not become a shift in *intensity*. Those qualities live almost entirely in direction, and a correction that cannot hold magnitude still while it turns will keep overshooting them — getting louder when you only wanted it darker. Still a frozen body with a removable correction, then, but a correction whose two hands are no longer tied together.

## The Adapter Family: One Idea Wearing Many Shapes

LoRA and DoRA are the two you will meet most, but they sit inside a wider family — LoHa, LoKr, IA3, prefix-tuning, bias-tuning, more arriving as you read. Do not memorise it as a catalogue of gadgets. They are variations on a single idea, distinguished by two questions: *where do you hook in,* and *what shape does the correction take?*

The first is the more musical, because *where* is usually treated as a technical default when it is really a choice. Recall from Chapter 5 what the two attentions *do*. A laminate bonded at the cross-attention door, where the prompt is heard, biases *how the words are interpreted* — it teaches prompt-hearing. A laminate bonded in the self-attention, where moments relate across time, biases *structure* — how a phrase answers an earlier one, how repetition and form cohere. (Others touch only the tiny bias terms, or prepend a few learned slots without altering any weight at all.) So the attachment point decides whether you teach the instrument a new *style*, a new *timbre*, or — most intriguingly — a new *responsiveness* to a signal it did not used to weigh.

The second is geometry: a narrow bottleneck (LoRA), a magnitude-and-direction split (DoRA), two patterns multiplied, a per-channel scaling (IA3) — each a different shape for the small change you are allowed.

Hold onto those two questions. They are not only how you place an adapter — they are the two you end up asking of *every* control method in this book. This family is your first rehearsal of a habit of thought that runs through everything that follows.

One wrinkle for the builder who wants precision. You met **adaLN** in Chapter 6 — the global, sigma-aware signal that re-voices every block as the material melts and sets. Where does a cross-attention laminate sit relative to it? Not in conflict: adaLN changes step to step as the carving proceeds, while the LoRA is a *fixed, local* bias welded into one matrix, the same at every sigma. The laminate changes *what the junction computes*; adaLN changes *how loudly that computation may speak* at this molten-ness. They layer — which is precisely why a LoRA's effect can feel stronger or weaker at different points in the sigma walk though the laminate itself never changed. The body is biased once; the voicing of that bias still breathes with the timestep.

## The Hinge

Everything in this chapter teaches the instrument's body a new **direction of adjustment**. A finetune moves the whole body that way permanently; a LoRA leans it reversibly; a DoRA leans it more faithfully; the wider family leans it from different attachment points with differently shaped hands. All of them answer the same question: *which way should the instrument tend to move, by default, before the first note sounds?*

There is one thing none of them can do, and naming it is the most important sentence in the chapter. A weight-change can teach the instrument a new *bias* — a lean, a preference, a default direction. It cannot teach it a new *sense*: it cannot make the body **responsive to a signal it has never been given**, cannot bolt on a fresh ear that listens, during the performance, for something you care about and corrects toward it in real time. A laminate changes how the instrument *tends*; it does not give it something new to *hear*.

That distinction — a direction of adjustment versus a new sense, a bias baked in versus a feedback loop added on — is the hinge the next chapter turns on. Cross it and things become possible no laminate can reach: an instrument that *tracks* a target you set while it plays, holding a brightness, following an envelope, correcting toward a loudness, rather than merely leaning toward a remembered style. We have learned to re-voice the body; we have not yet learned to give it a sense it lacked. Hold the difference — it is about to do a great deal of work.

## Openings

- If two laminates trained on different material can be blended mid-performance, can the space *between* them hold a voice neither could reach alone — and is there a difference between a sound the model *learned* and one that lives only in the interpolation between two corrections it never saw together?
- *Where* an adapter attaches changes what it can teach. If you wanted to teach not a style but a *responsiveness* — a predisposition to react to a signal the base model currently ignores — where would you bond the laminate, and how would you even know you had succeeded?
- DoRA separates magnitude from direction. Are there musical qualities that live almost entirely in *direction* — a re-colouring with no change in force — such that a correction blind to the split would chase them and never catch them? And is there a converse, qualities of *pure magnitude*?
- When you stack a set-and-forget laminate with a measure-and-correct control head in one run — a frozen lean and a live feedback loop pressing on the same latent — does the baked-in bias help the head reach its target faster, or fight its corrections at every step?
- Every method here teaches a direction the body will *tend* toward by default. Which musical intentions are fundamentally about a *tendency* — a habit, a default reach — and which are really about *listening and correcting in the moment*, and therefore cannot be a laminate at all, no matter how cleverly shaped?
