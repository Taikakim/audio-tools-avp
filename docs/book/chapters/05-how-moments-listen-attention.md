# Chapter 5 - How Moments Listen: Attention as Form

Imagine you are sitting in the middle of a band that has been playing together for years. You are the drummer. Without turning your head, you know exactly where the bassist's root note landed two bars ago, you feel the guitarist already leaning toward the turnaround that's coming, and somewhere underneath all of it you are still keeping faith with the very first downbeat — the one that told everyone what kind of night this was going to be. You are not consulting a memory of these things. You are *hearing* them, all at once, as a single field of relationship. That is what lets you play form rather than merely play notes.

A cheap machine cannot do this. A simple processor hears the present sample, acts, and forgets. It has no faculty for relationship across time, so it cannot build a chorus, lock a groove, or recognise that bar thirty-three is a return of bar one. What sets the generative carver of SA3 apart — the diffusion transformer at the heart of the instrument — is that it has a faculty for exactly this kind of listening. It is called **attention**, and it is the closest thing the instrument has to an internal ear for its own form.

This chapter is about two ears. One points inward, at the music's own material. One points outward, at your words. They are different faculties, they live at different junctions inside the instrument, and — this is the part that should make a builder's hands itch — both are places you can reach in and put your hands.

## The Inward Ear: Self-Attention

Recall from Chapter 2 that the carver never works on raw audio. It works on the latent — a stream of slices, roughly ten and a half per second, each one a column of numbers standing in for the resonant signature of that moment. And recall from Chapter 3 that generation is a walk: many passes, each one re-reading the whole block and deciding where to cut next.

Here is the crucial thing about that re-reading. At every step, every slice of the latent is allowed to *consult every other slice*. Not just its neighbours — every one of them, the whole length of the clip, past and future alike, since all the slices exist simultaneously during carving. A slice near the end of the piece can reach back and ask the opening slice, "what kind of thing are you?" — and shape itself in light of the answer. This is **self-attention**: the music's moments listening to one another.

It helps to be precise about what is exchanged, so the metaphor does not float off into poetry. Each moment produces, from its own contents, a small set of vectors — directions in the latent's space. One of them acts like a *question* (a learned pattern the moment is searching for), another like an *answer* (a learned pattern the moment is broadcasting about itself). The mechanism compares every moment's question against every other moment's answer; where a question and an answer point the same way, that is a strong match, and the questioning moment draws material from the moment it matched. A slice in the second chorus carries a question that the first chorus answers loudly; so the second chorus leans into the first, inheriting its shape. A backbeat carries a question that the other backbeats answer; so the groove coheres, beat reinforcing beat. None of this is stored anywhere as a "chorus memory" or a "groove rule." It is re-derived, freshly, every single step, by moments interrogating moments.

That is why it is fair to say self-attention is the **acoustic body of form itself**. Form, in music, is not a property any single instant possesses. A note is not "a chorus." A chorus is a *relationship* — a kinship between stretches of time that recognise each other. Repetition, call-and-response, the arc of a build, the satisfaction of a return: every one of these is a pattern of which-moments-lean-on-which. Self-attention is the faculty that holds those leanings. It is less like a tape that remembers and more like the live, mutual awareness in a tight ensemble, where everyone is shaping their part by everyone else's, continuously.

There is one thing this faculty cannot do on its own: it cannot tell *how far apart* two moments sit in time. Matching questions to answers is timeless — the opening and the coda meet as equals. The sense of *distance* between moments arrives through a separate mechanism, the relative time-sense you will meet in Chapter 7. Treat that as the other half of the complete picture: this chapter is where moments meet; Chapter 7 is how they know the span between them.

## The Outward Ear: Cross-Attention

Now the second ear. Everything so far has been the music listening to itself. But the carver also has to obey you — to attend to the prompt you typed. That is a fundamentally different act of listening, because the thing being listened to is not part of the music at all. It is outside instruction.

This is where a great deal of mystified thinking creeps in. People imagine the prompt's influence as a kind of perfume — diffused everywhere, soaked into every corner of the model, impossible to point at. It is not. The words enter the instrument at a **specific, locatable junction**, and nowhere else.

Here is the path. Your phrase is handed to the conditioner — the text encoder, which Chapter 8 examines in full — and comes back not as words but as a sequence of dense vectors, the translated shadows of what you said. Those vectors then wait at the door. And at certain layers of the carver, interleaved with the self-attention layers, sits a second kind of attention: **cross-attention**. Here the carver's moments pose their questions not to each other but *across*, to the waiting prompt-vectors. Each moment of the music asks, in effect, "of everything the words are offering, what is relevant to me?" — and draws from the prompt accordingly.

That word *across* is the whole point. Self-attention is the music consulting itself; cross-attention is the music consulting the words. The second ear is a *door with a frame* — a named place in the architecture where outside instruction is admitted into the carving. Words steer sound here, and nowhere else. If you wanted to stand at the exact spot where language becomes music, you would stand at the cross-attention layers, and you would not have to stand anywhere else.

It is worth letting that sink in, because it overturns a common feeling that prompting is a vague, holistic spell cast over the whole system. The *effect* of a prompt may be holistic — a mood colours everything — but the *point of entry* is sharp and singular. A luthier who can name the junction can work the junction.

## Both Ears Are Control Surfaces

This is where the chapter earns its place in a book about putting your hands in. Attention is not only how the instrument works; it is a surface with grain and texture that a builder can shape. Three handholds, roughly in order of how directly they touch what we have just described. Each one occupies a different slot on the map of control we will lay out fully in Chapter 14 — a different *where*, a different *when*, a different breadth — and it is worth feeling those differences now, before the map names them.

**Widening or narrowing the door.** The cross-attention junction is where the prompt's authority physically lands, and that authority is not a fixed quantity. There is a real, operable sense in which the door can be opened wider, so the music leans harder on the words, or narrowed, so it consults them more loosely. Be concrete about what "wider" and "narrower" do, because Chapter 9 will lean on it: opening the door does not amplify every prompt feature evenly. It sharpens the contrast — the features the words clearly call for get pushed harder, and features the words are silent or ambivalent about get pushed *away*. Narrow the door and the opposite happens: the music consults the words gently, conflicting instructions blur together rather than fighting, and the base instrument's own tendencies have more room. You have already met the most familiar version of this dial without knowing where it lived: classifier-free guidance, the subject of Chapter 9, is in effect a way of amplifying the gap between music-that-heard-the-words and music-that-did-not. Now you know the doorway it is amplifying *through*. Guidance is a volume knob on this specific ear. It is an inference-time, set-once-per-run control with holistic reach — exactly the opposite corner of the map from the surgical heads to come.

**Teaching the door new manners.** Because cross-attention is a definite location in the instrument's body, it is somewhere you can *attach* a trained correction — a small adapter, of the kind whose taxonomy Chapter 11 lays out, bonded right at this junction. Such an adapter does not rewrite the whole instrument; it biases, at the door, *which* features of the prompt the music leans toward. Imagine a thin shim fitted precisely at the threshold so that certain instructions are heard more sympathetically than the base instrument would hear them — a learned predisposition to listen for, say, rhythmic words over textural ones, or to take a class of descriptions more literally. Where does such a manner come from? You would teach it the way Chapter 11 describes: with a modest pile of examples that *already* embody the bias you want — clips paired with the kinds of prompt the base model mishears — and a training signal that rewards the adapter for nudging the door's listening toward what those examples actually contain. The full account belongs to Chapter 11. What belongs *here* is the recognition that the cross-attention junction is a mounting point — a place engineered, almost, to receive new responsiveness — and that an adapter bonded here is itself a kind of control head, attached at an attention surface rather than read off the bare latent (the distinction Chapter 13 draws out).

**Masking who hears whom.** The inward ear, too, is workable. Self-attention's rule is "every moment may consult every other moment" — but that *may* is a permission, and permissions can be edited. A mask is simply a decision, slice by slice, about which consultations are allowed: you can forbid certain moments from hearing certain others. Mask the second verse from hearing the first, and the kinship that would have made it a *return* is severed — the model can no longer recognise the verse as a homecoming. But what emotional shape arrives in its place? Something that no longer knows it was supposed to come home; a passage that re-states without re-meaning. You sculpt structure by selective deafening. This is a cousin of the inpainting and clamping in Chapter 10, but aimed at the faculty of relationship rather than at the material itself — and like an adapter at the door, it is a place where a control head could in principle attach, deciding mid-carve which kinships to permit (again, Chapter 13).

Hold the honest line through all three. The first two junctions are literal and workable: guidance on the door is well-trodden and dependable, and adapters at the door are established practice whose *musical* targeting is still an open craft. The third — masking the inward ear — is mechanically real but largely untested as a deliberate compositional tool, precisely because form is subtle and the consequences of cutting a connection are not always where you expect them. The surface is real, and it has texture; what nobody has yet mapped is what music it yields. Knowing which is which is part of knowing the instrument.

## What Else Could Walk Through the Door

I want to end by pressing on the cross-attention doorway one more time, because it opens onto the largest country in this book.

We have described that door as the place where *words* enter. But notice what the door actually requires: not language, but vectors — a sequence of numerical shapes that the carver's moments can pose their questions to. The conditioner happens to produce those shapes from text. The door does not know that. It knows only how to let the music consult *something* laid before it.

So the real question is not "how do I word the prompt better?" That question belongs to Chapter 8. The question this chapter leaves you with is stranger and more generative: *what else could you lay before that door?* If a sequence of vectors can pass through, and the carver will dutifully consult it at every layer, then text is merely the first and most obvious thing to send. A measured musical contour over time. The output of a control head. Some shape you have not named yet. The doorway is established; what walks through it is, increasingly, up to the builder. Asking "what else could walk through the door?" is exactly the move Chapter 16 will ask you to make again and again, at every junction in the instrument — and now you have done it once, here, at the one place where instruction becomes sound.

## Openings

- If form lives in moments listening to each other, what happens when you deliberately prevent certain moments from hearing certain others? Could you compose large-scale structure by *deafening* — severing the kinships that would otherwise produce a return — and what would that sound like as it failed?
- The cross-attention door requires vectors, not words. What else could you walk through it — a brightness contour over time, a rhythmic-density curve, the running report of a control head — and what would it cost in fidelity to send something the door was never trained to expect?
- An adapter bonded at the cross-attention junction can bias which prompt-features the music leans toward. What musical qualities are currently unreachable by words alone — things you can hear in your head but cannot make the prompt deliver — and is the door the right place to teach them?
- The self-attention weights are themselves a kind of live score of mutual relevance: a map of which moments the model currently thinks belong together. What would it mean to *read that map back out* as a signal — to see, mid-carve, what structure the instrument already believes it is building?
- We have two ears pointed at two things: the music's own material and your words. Is there a third thing worth pointing an ear at — and if so, would you build it as a new doorway, or teach one of the existing ears to listen differently?
