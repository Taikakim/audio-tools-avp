# Chapter 8 - The Voice: Prompt and Conditioner

Think of the text prompt as a singer humming an instruction to a skilled session band that has never met them. Play them a feeling and they give you something plausible in the style you named — but that plausibility is the whole of what the humming can guarantee. A conductor who says "melancholy, sparse, late-night" has tilted every player's instincts without specifying which note anyone plays. The room responds; the notes are theirs.

The prompt is the control nearly everyone reaches for first and almost nobody outgrows: the most direct voice the instrument has, and also the bluntest — and the second fact is the reason the rest of this book exists.

## The translator in the signal chain

Your words do not enter the carving process as words. Between the phrase you type and the diffusion transformer that does the carving sits a translator: a text encoder called T5-Gemma, itself a large language model, whose job is to read your phrase and emit a sequence of dense vectors — ordered bundles of numbers, roughly one per token. By the time anything musical happens, those vectors are all that remains.

Picture this the way a recording engineer pictures a signal chain. You speak into a microphone; what travels down the cable is no longer your voice but a voltage that stands in for it, shaped by the character of the microphone. T5-Gemma is that microphone for language, with its own idea of what "warm" and "driving" and "lo-fi" mean, learned from an enormous corpus of text. Two prompts that look similar to you may translate to vectors pointing in very different directions; two that read differently may collapse to nearly the same.

A builder should know that grain's bias the way an organ-builder knows that a given rank of pipes speaks brilliantly in its home register and goes muddy at the extremes. The encoder learned language overwhelmingly from the text that surrounds recorded music on the open web: genre tags, production vocabulary, the conventional descriptors of conventional music. So "warm," "punchy," "lo-fi," "vintage analog" survive translation beautifully. The starvation is at the edges: a non-Western tuning, a microtonal interval named precisely, a rhythmic cycle that is not a small power of two, an instrument the streaming world rarely tags. Name one and the encoder still produces *some* vector, but a vague one, pointing toward the nearest thing it knows — often a Western approximation of what you meant. It will not refuse you; it will quietly round you off toward its training.

So ask, of any word you reach for: does this dimension of my intention translate well, or is it one the corpus starves? "Warm" survives. "Narrow," meant as a precise spectral or stereo width, very likely does not — it lands fuzzier, because the word is used loosely and the encoder never learned to point it sharply. Knowing which of your intentions will survive the cable and which will be rounded is among the most practical things you can carry into a prompt. The encoder sits between your intention and the sound, and you do not get to bypass it.

## Where the prompt sits, and how deep

Where do those vectors do their work? They enter the diffusion transformer through the cross-attention door of Chapter 5 — *How Moments Listen* — the second ear the carver opens, not toward its own forming material but toward your translated phrase. The doorway's workings belong to that chapter; here it is enough to know it is a real, located junction, not a vague influence sprinkled everywhere. Every layer can consult the vectors, but it consults them *there*.

And the door is repeated, cut into many layers, shallow and deep. The shallow layers, near the input, handle coarse and global decisions: the overall material, the broad gesture. The deep layers refine surface and detail. So the prompt's influence is graded — a coarse word like "orchestral" finds purchase early, a fine word like "shimmering" finds it late. You do not command which layer hears which word; the network distributes that itself.

There is a second axis of time, distinct from depth. The prompt is consulted at *every carving step*, but its grip is not equal across them, because the material's molten-ness changes underneath it. That changing temperature, and the dial that governs it, belong to Chapter 4 — *Sigma: The One Dial That Says How Molten*. Its consequence for the voice: the prompt's authority is strongest over coarse form while things are hot, narrowing to surface refinement as they set — which is why prompting *feels* different depending on when its authority lands.

The prompt reaches the model *only* through the cross-attention door. It cannot reach the self-attention layers — where, as Chapter 5 showed, moments of the forming audio listen to *each other*, and so where repetition, phrase structure, and large-scale form are decided. The words shape what each moment is *about*; they do not enter the room where moments relate across time. Rhythm and large-scale architecture come from the model's own self-attention and its handful of memory tokens. So when you want to govern *structure* — "return to this figure every eight bars," "build, then drop" — and the prompt keeps failing you, the fault is not poor words. You are speaking into the wrong door, and no vocabulary will move its seat.

## A richer slot than it looks

Here is the turn that makes this chapter matter for a builder. The thing the model receives is not text — it is a slot *filled* by text-derived vectors, and the model was trained to expect a certain shape of conditioning there and to make sound that honors it. Whether the shape arrived from the word "melancholy" or from somewhere else is, to the carver, beside the point. It reads the vectors.

That distinction — between the prompt as words and the prompt as a *conditioning shape* — is the first crack of daylight into deeper control. Fill that slot, or one beside it, with vectors derived from something other than language, and the model steers accordingly, provided it was taught to read what you put there. The conditioning stream is a port; text is the cable currently plugged in. What *else* might be plugged in, and at what cost in retraining, runs through the entire second half of this book.

## The duration conditioner: a number that the model obeys

The quietest part of the instrument's input is the most instructive. Alongside the text encoder, SA3 accepts a second conditioning signal that is not language at all: the intended duration of the piece, in seconds, supplied as a plain number. It does not pass through T5-Gemma, nor is it described in words like "make it about thirty seconds long." It is *given* — a measured quantity, folded into the conditioning stream in a numeric form the model was trained to obey, and it obeys deeply. The model does not merely shape the arc of energy toward a logical ending; it makes *structural* decisions as a function of intended length — where the hook sits, how often a pattern repeats, how much room to leave for an intro. A thirty-second and a hundred-and-twenty-second generation of the *same prompt* are materially different pieces, not the same music stretched to fit, because the model planned around the number from the first molten step.

This is a proof of concept. A musical quantity, duration, was made *first-class*: not approximated through prose or coaxed out by clever wording, but handed to the model directly as a number it learned to honor — a dedicated channel for one measurable property, and it works.

So ask the question this whole book is built to let you ask well: *what other measurable quantity could be given the same kind of channel?* Brightness. Rhythmic density. The energy in the low end. The rate of events per bar. Any property you can measure is, in principle, a candidate for its own knob wired into the conditioning stream — **if the model were trained to accept it**. That conditional is load-bearing. Duration works only because the model was trained, from the start, with duration as an input; the channel and the obedience are learned together. You cannot invent a new numeric input on a model that never saw one.

That single constraint forks the road. If you are willing to *train*, you can grow a new native channel and the model will obey your number the way it already obeys duration — the work of Chapter 16. But to govern a quantity *without* retraining, there is another route. Control heads measure at runtime instead: a small separate net reads the forming latent, reports the quantity, and you nudge the material to close the gap to your target — all on a model that never learned about your quantity. That is Chapter 13. Duration, sitting right inside the input panel of the instrument as it ships, is the seed of both branches.

## What words reach, and what they do not

A prompt's strengths and failures fall along a line you can learn to feel. Words are extraordinary at *holistic* steering — qualities that name a region of musical space rather than a point: genre, mood, instrumentation, era, texture, production aesthetic. Say "warm analog soul ballad, brushed drums, late sixties" and the band knows exactly which room you mean, and walks into it. These are the dimensions language was built to carry, where the encoder's corpus is richest.

Words fail at *precision* — at placing a single note, fixing an exact timing, specifying a harmonic move, dictating the decay of a particular snare. Ask for "punchy drums" and you land in that neighborhood reliably. You cannot ask for a transient that lasts twelve milliseconds and rolls off above eight kilohertz — not because the words are forbidden, but because they do not *resolve* to those things on the way through. "Punchy" is a direction in vector space; "twelve milliseconds" is not a place the encoder has a vector to point at — and even if it did, the cross-attention door is too coarse a seat from which to govern a single transient's edge.

Here is a failure mode subtle enough to mislead you. Say your intention is *attack* — sharp transient response, sound that snaps. You reach for "punchy, crisp, bright." But the encoder may map those words to *timbre* — spectral color — rather than to *envelope shape*. So you get exactly the color you named, a bright, present, crisp-sounding mix, while the dimension you cared about — onset sharpness, the envelope's leading edge — goes ungoverned: the transients may still be soft, the attacks rounded. The prompt is fluent in naming a feeling and mute in governing a dimension.

The conductor again: "brighter, more urgent" tilts the whole orchestra, but "second oboe, clip your release on beat three" needs a hand on that one player. The gap between what a word *names* and what it *governs* is exactly the territory the deeper tools of this book occupy — the control heads of Chapter 13 reach into the precision gap that words cannot cross, and the numeric channels of Chapter 16 build the dedicated knobs that "punchy" only gestures toward.

The craft of prompting deserves its own guide, and we leave it to one: the things you can *do* at the input — negative prompts, layering several prompts, running a batch of variations, the slow work of vocabulary tuning — are all worth learning, but they sit beside our concern. Here we care about the prompt as an architectural site: where it sits, how it translates, what it reaches, and where it stops.

## Two cables into the material

The prompt is not the only channel into the forming material. When you seed generation from a real recording — re-melting an existing sound rather than starting from pure noise (the SDEdit route, Chapter 9) — and *also* give a prompt, you have plugged two genuinely separate cables into the same instrument. The seed supplies the starting material, already partly shaped, carrying its own form and gesture; the prompt supplies a steering force on top of it. In conflict, neither simply overrides the other — they *negotiate*, and where they land depends on how far you re-melted the seed: a molten seed gives the prompt more room, a barely-warmed seed holds its own form against the words. Chapter 9 takes up how they balance.

## The pull exists; the dial comes later

Everything above establishes that the prompt *pulls* — that those translated vectors exert a real, directional force on the carving. What it does not establish is how *hard* you can lean on that pull. That amplification dial — classifier-free guidance — is Chapter 9. The prompt is no suggestion the model is free to ignore; it is a steering force entering at a known door, and how loudly to turn it up is a separate act of voicing.

## Openings

- If the model receives *vectors*, not words, what else could be shaped into vectors and fed to that same slot — a chord chart, a reference recording's character, a contour drawn by hand — and what would it take to teach the model to read them as fluently as it reads language?
- Duration proves a measured number can be first-class conditioning. Which musical quantities are measurable and stable enough across styles to deserve their own numeric channel — and who gets to decide what the instrument is trained to obey?
- The encoder is a translator with its own grain, rich where its corpus was rich and starved at the edges. Could you ever know what your prompt actually *became* on the other side of it — and which of your intentions are most likely to be rounded off before they reach the sound?
- The prompt enters only through cross-attention — it shapes what moments are *about*, never how they listen to each other. If structure lives behind a door words cannot reach, what *would* a usable handle on it look like — a second kind of prompt, a sketched arc, a borrowed skeleton — and would governing form from outside cost you the coherence self-attention gives for free?
- The prompt is fixed once, before the first step, and consulted unchanged thereafter. If it could instead be *changed* mid-generation — faded from one phrase to another, or switched at a chosen molten-ness — what would you most want to say to the early, molten material that you would not say to the same piece once it had nearly set?
