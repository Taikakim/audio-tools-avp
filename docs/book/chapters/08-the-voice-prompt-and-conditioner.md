# Chapter 8 - The Voice: Prompt and Conditioner

Think of the text prompt as a singer humming an instruction to a session band that has never met them. The band is extraordinarily skilled. Play them a feeling and they will give you something musical, coherent, and plausible in the style you named. But "plausible in the style you named" is the whole of what the humming can guarantee. A conductor who turns to the strings and says "melancholy, sparse, late-night" has shaped a room — has tilted every player's instincts in a direction — without once specifying which note anyone plays. The room responds. The notes are theirs.

This is the prompt's nature, and it is worth getting clear about early, because the prompt is the control nearly everyone reaches for first and the one almost nobody outgrows. It is the most direct voice the instrument has. It is also the bluntest. Both of those facts are true at once, and the second one is the reason the rest of this book exists.

## The translator in the signal chain

Your words do not enter the carving process as words. Between the phrase you type and the diffusion transformer that does the carving sits a translator: a text encoder called T5-Gemma, itself a large language model, whose entire job is to read your phrase and emit a sequence of dense vectors — ordered bundles of numbers, one bundle for roughly each token of your prompt. By the time anything musical happens, those vectors are all that remains. The words themselves are gone.

It helps to picture this the way a recording engineer pictures a signal chain. You speak into a microphone; what travels down the cable is no longer your voice but a voltage that stands in for it — a faithful translation, but a translation, shaped by the character of the microphone that made it. T5-Gemma is that microphone for language. It has its own grain, its own sensitivities, its own idea of what "warm" and "driving" and "lo-fi" mean, learned from an enormous corpus of text. Two prompts that look similar to you may translate to vectors that point in surprisingly different directions; two that read differently may collapse to nearly the same shape.

That grain is not neutral, and a builder should know its bias the way an organ-builder knows that a given rank of pipes speaks brilliantly in its home register and goes muddy and indistinct at the extremes of its scale. The encoder learned language from text — overwhelmingly the kind that surrounds recorded music on the open web: streaming-service genre tags, production vocabulary, the conventional descriptors of conventional music. So its vectors are richest exactly where that corpus is rich. "Warm," "punchy," "lo-fi," "vintage analog" survive translation beautifully, because thousands of writers have used them about thousands of recordings and the encoder has a deep, well-worn channel for each. The starvation is at the edges: a particular non-Western tuning, a microtonal interval named precisely, a rhythmic cycle that is not a small power of two, an instrument the streaming world rarely tags. Name one and the encoder still produces *some* vector — it always does — but a vague one, pointing toward the nearest thing it knows well, often a Western approximation of what you meant. The instrument will not refuse you. It will quietly round you off toward its training.

So ask, of any word you reach for: does this dimension of my intention translate well, or is it one the corpus starves? "Warm" survives. "Narrow," meant as a precise spectral or stereo width, very likely does not — it lands somewhere fuzzier, because the word is used loosely across the corpus and the encoder never learned to point it sharply. Knowing *which* of your intentions are likely to survive the cable and which are likely to be rounded is among the most practical things you can carry into a prompt. The encoder's understanding of language sits between your intention and the sound, and you do not get to bypass it.

## Where the prompt sits, and how deep

Where do those vectors actually do their work? They enter the diffusion transformer through the cross-attention door of Chapter 5 — *How Moments Listen* — the second ear the carver opens, not toward its own forming material but toward your translated phrase. That chapter is the proper home for how the doorway functions; here it is enough to know that it is a real, located junction, not a vague influence sprinkled everywhere. The prompt steers from a fixed seat in the architecture. Every carving step, every layer of the model can turn and consult the vectors, but it consults them *there*, through that door.

And the door is not in one place — it is repeated, cut into many layers of the network, shallow and deep. This matters more than it first appears. The shallow layers, near the input, tend to handle coarse and global decisions: the overall material, the broad gesture. The deep layers refine surface and detail. So the prompt's influence is not a single push but a graded one — a coarse word like "orchestral" finds purchase early, a fine word like "shimmering" finds it late. You do not command which layer hears which word; the network distributes that itself. But it is useful to feel that "the prompt" is really the same vectors consulted at many altitudes, each layer taking what its altitude is built to use.

There is a second axis of time, distinct from depth, and it is one of the most important things to feel about prompting. The prompt is consulted at *every carving step* — but its grip is not equal across them, because the material's molten-ness changes underneath it. That changing temperature, and the single dial that governs it, belong to Chapter 4 — *Sigma: The One Dial That Says How Molten*. What matters here is only its consequence for the voice: the prompt's authority shifts with how molten the material is, strongest over coarse form while things are hot, narrowing to mere surface refinement as they set. The same words exert a different force on a piece that has barely begun to take shape than on one nearly committed — which is why prompting *feels* different depending on when its authority happens to land.

One more fact about the seat, and it is a boundary worth marking clearly. The prompt reaches the model *only* through the cross-attention door. It cannot reach the self-attention layers — the place, as Chapter 5 showed, where moments of the forming audio listen to *each other* and so where repetition, phrase structure, and large-scale form are decided. The words shape what each moment is *about*; they do not enter the room where moments relate across time. Rhythm and large-scale architecture come from the model's own self-attention and its handful of memory tokens, not from your prompt. So when you want to govern *structure* — "return to this figure every eight bars," "build, then drop" — and the prompt keeps failing you, it is not that you chose poor words. You are speaking into the wrong door. The prompt was never seated where structure is decided, and no vocabulary will move its seat.

## A richer slot than it looks

Here is the turn that makes this chapter matter for a builder rather than merely a user. The thing the model actually receives is not text. It is a slot that happens to be *filled* by text-derived vectors. The model was trained to expect a certain shape of conditioning arriving at that slot, and to make sound that honors it. Whether the shape arrived from the word "melancholy" or from somewhere else is, to the carver, beside the point. It reads the vectors. It responds to the vectors.

That distinction — between the prompt as words and the prompt as a *conditioning shape* — is the first crack of daylight into deeper control. If you could fill that slot, or a slot beside it, with vectors derived from something other than language, the model would steer accordingly, provided it had been taught to read what you put there. The conditioning stream is a port. Text is the cable currently plugged into it. The question of what *else* might be plugged in, and at what cost in retraining, runs through the entire second half of this book.

## The duration conditioner: a number that the model obeys

Sit, for a moment, with the quietest part of the instrument's input, because it is the most instructive thing in this chapter.

Alongside the text encoder, SA3 accepts a second conditioning signal that is not language at all: the intended duration of the piece, in seconds, supplied as a plain number. It does not pass through T5-Gemma. It is not described in words like "make it about thirty seconds long." It is *given* — a measured quantity, folded into the conditioning stream in a numeric form the model was trained to obey. And obey it does, but not in the shallow way you might expect. The model does not merely shape the arc of energy toward a logical ending. It makes *structural* decisions as a function of intended length — where the hook sits, how often a pattern repeats before it moves on, how much room to leave for an intro before the piece commits. A thirty-second generation and a hundred-and-twenty-second generation of the *same prompt* are materially different pieces, not the same music stretched to fit. The number reaches deeper into the form than any post-hoc time-stretch could, because the model planned around it from the first molten step.

Notice what just happened, because it is a proof of concept hiding in plain sight. A musical quantity — duration — was made *first-class*: not approximated through prose, not coaxed out by clever wording, but handed to the model directly as a number it learned to honor. The model has a dedicated channel for one measurable property of the output, and that channel works.

Now ask the obvious next question, the one this whole book is built to let you ask well: *what other measurable quantity could be given the same kind of channel?* Brightness. Rhythmic density. The energy living in the low end. The rate of events per bar. Any property you can measure with a number is, in principle, a candidate for its own numeric conditioning input — its own dedicated knob wired into the conditioning stream — **if the model were trained to accept it**. That conditional is load-bearing. Duration works only because the model was trained, from the start, with duration as an input; the channel and the obedience are learned together. You cannot invent a new numeric input on a model that never saw one in training and expect it to listen.

That single constraint forks the road. If you are willing to *train*, you can grow a new native channel and the model will obey your number the way it already obeys duration — this is the work of Chapter 16, which returns to exactly this question: what controls should you invent next, and which measurable quantities deserve their own wire? But if you want a quantity governed *without* retraining the instrument, there is another route entirely. Control heads sidestep the training requirement by measuring at runtime rather than expecting pre-trained obedience: a small separate net reads the forming latent, reports the quantity, and you nudge the material to close the gap to your target — all at inference time, on a model that never learned about your quantity at all. That is Chapter 13. Duration is the seed of both branches. One root — a measured number made first-class — sitting right inside the input panel of the instrument as it ships.

## What words reach, and what they do not

A prompt's strengths and its failures are not random; they fall along a line you can learn to feel. Words are extraordinary at *holistic* steering — the qualities that name a region of musical space rather than a point: genre, mood, instrumentation, era, texture, production aesthetic. Say "warm analog soul ballad, brushed drums, late sixties" and the band knows exactly which room you mean, and walks into it. These are the dimensions language was built to carry, and the dimensions the encoder's corpus is richest in.

Words fail at *precision* — at placing a single note, fixing an exact timing, specifying a harmonic move, dictating the decay of a particular snare. You can ask for "punchy drums" and land in that neighborhood, reliably. You cannot ask for a transient that lasts twelve milliseconds and rolls off above eight kilohertz — not because the words are forbidden, but because they do not *resolve* to those things on the way through. "Punchy" is a direction in vector space; "twelve milliseconds" is not a place the encoder has a vector to point at, and even if it did, the cross-attention door is too coarse a seat from which to govern a single transient's edge.

And here is a failure mode subtle enough to mislead you, worth holding onto because it teaches the whole lesson at once. Say your intention is *attack* — sharp transient response, sound that snaps. You reach for "punchy, crisp, bright." But the encoder may map those words to *timbre* — spectral color — rather than to *envelope shape*. So you get exactly the color you named: a bright, present, crisp-sounding mix. What you do not get is the timing you intended — the transients may still be soft, the attacks rounded, the snap absent under all that brightness. You named a feeling; the instrument gave you the nearest *color* to that feeling, because color is what its vectors point at well, and left the *dimension you actually cared about* — onset sharpness, the envelope's leading edge — ungoverned. This is the gap between naming a feeling and governing a dimension. The prompt is fluent in the first and mute in the second.

This is the conductor's verbal direction again: "brighter, more urgent" reliably tilts the whole orchestra, but "second oboe, clip your release on beat three" needs a hand on that one player, at that one moment. The gap between what a word *names* and what it *governs* is exactly the territory the deeper tools of this book are built to occupy — the control heads of Chapter 13 reach into the precision gap that words cannot cross, and the new numeric channels of Chapter 16 build the dedicated knobs that "punchy" only gestures toward.

The craft of prompting deserves its own guide and we leave it to one: the things you can *do* at the input — negative prompts that name what to steer away from, layering several prompts together, running a batch of variations from one phrase, the slow work of vocabulary tuning — are all real and all worth learning, but they sit beside our concern rather than inside it. Here we care about the prompt as an architectural site: where it sits, how it translates, what it reaches, and where it stops.

## Two cables into the material

Before leaving the conditioner, set down one more fact you will need shortly. The prompt is not the only channel into the forming material. When you seed generation from a real recording — re-melting an existing sound rather than starting from pure noise (the SDEdit route, Chapter 9) — and *also* give a prompt, you have plugged two cables into the same instrument at once. They do not fight for a single input; they are genuinely separate channels. The seed supplies the starting material, already partly shaped, carrying its own form and gesture; the prompt supplies a steering force on top of it. In conflict, neither simply overrides the other — they *negotiate*, and where they land depends on how far you re-melted the seed: a molten seed gives the prompt more room, a barely-warmed seed holds its own form against the words. For now, just register that the prompt is one channel among several into the material, not the sole authority over it. Chapter 9 takes up how those channels balance.

## The pull exists; the dial comes later

One last thing to set down, lightly, because it belongs to the next chapter. Everything above establishes that the prompt *pulls* — that those translated vectors exert a real, directional force on the carving. What it does not establish is how *hard* you can choose to lean on that pull. That amplification dial — classifier-free guidance — is Chapter 9. For now, simply register that the pull is genuine and locatable. The prompt is not a suggestion the model is free to ignore; it is a steering force entering at a known door. How loudly to turn it up is a separate act of voicing, and it has its own chapter.

## Openings

- If the model truly receives *vectors* and not words, what else could be shaped into vectors and fed to that same slot — a chord chart, a reference recording's character, a contour drawn by hand — and what would it take to teach the model to read them as fluently as it reads language?
- Duration proves a measured number can be first-class conditioning. Which musical quantities are already measurable enough, and stable enough across styles, to deserve their own numeric channel — and who gets to decide what the instrument is trained to obey?
- The encoder is a translator with its own grain, rich where its corpus was rich and starved at the edges. Could you ever know what your prompt actually *became* on the other side of it — and which of your intentions are most likely to be rounded off toward the encoder's training before they ever reach the sound?
- The prompt enters only through cross-attention, never self-attention — it shapes what moments are *about*, never how they listen to each other. If structure lives behind a door words cannot reach, what *would* a usable handle on it even look like — a second kind of prompt, a sketched arc, a borrowed skeleton from another piece — and would governing form from outside cost you the very coherence self-attention gives for free?
- The prompt is fixed once, before the first step, and consulted unchanged thereafter. If it could instead be *changed* mid-generation — faded smoothly from one phrase to another, or switched at a chosen molten-ness — what would you most want to say to the early, molten material that you would not say to the same piece once it had nearly set?
