# Chapter 3 - Carving Order from Noise

Here is what is not happening when Stable Audio 3 makes music: a single, decisive pour. Raw material slung into a mould, the mould struck off, the casting left to set into whatever shape the mould happened to hold. That image is wrong in every part that matters, and unlearning it is the first work of this chapter.

Here is what is happening instead. Picture a stone carver standing before a rough block. They do not know, at the outset, exactly what the finished form will be — only the direction of it, a pressure of intention. They take a pass with the chisel, step back, and read what is now there: this plane has opened, that mass still crowds, the grain runs unexpectedly here. They take another pass, informed by the first. And another. The form emerges not from a decision made once at the beginning but from a long sequence of small readings and small cuts, each responding to the state the previous cut left behind. When it is done it feels inevitable — as though it had been waiting in the stone all along — but nothing was waiting. It was found, step by step, by a hand that kept asking the same question: given what is here now, where do I cut next?

That is the generative act in SA3. Hold onto the carver; almost everything in this book is painted on the canvas this chapter stretches.

## Starting from nothing

The carver at least begins with stone — a block with real structure, grain, density, flaws. SA3 begins with something far emptier: pure noise. A latent stream (the compact inner material of Chapter 2) filled with no pitch, no pulse, no timbre, no direction whatsoever. Random numbers, the audio equivalent of television static or the hiss between radio stations. There is nothing in it. That emptiness is not a defect to be overcome; it is the honest starting condition — the bare cane blank before the reed-voicer's knife has touched it, still capable of becoming any voice at all.

From that nothing, order has to be *carved*. Not filtered out of an existing signal — there is no signal yet. Not selected from a menu of stored songs — nothing is stored. Built, progressively, from static into structure. The astonishing fact you should let sit with you for a moment is that coherent music can be walked out of pure randomness at all.

And here is the seed of your very first handhold, before any of the famous dials. *Which* noise you start from is itself a choice. Two different draws of static, carved under the very same prompt with the very same settings, arrive at two different pieces — related in style, distinct in their particulars. The starting noise is a kind of prior, a faint set of initial leanings that the carving will amplify into specifics. Builders call fixing it the *seed*. It is the zeroth control surface: the one you touch before you have touched anything else, the grain of the blank block. You will not steer with it precisely — it is far too tangled for that — but know it is there, the first place your hand rests on the instrument.

The model does not read this blank block in isolation. It also reads your text prompt (Chapter 8); together, the prompt and the material's current state determine which way the guide points.

And this seed-setting is the zero point of a continuum. Chapter 9 explores starting instead from a real recording, re-melted to a chosen degree of chaos, and carving from there — a technique called SDEdit. Pure noise is one end of that scale; a barely-disturbed recording is the other; everything between is yours to choose.

## A velocity is a direction, not a speed

Now to the engine of the walk. At each step the model is shown the material in its current half-formed state and asked one question: which way should this move to become more like real music? Its answer is called a *velocity*.

That word can mislead a musician, so let us be precise about it. This velocity is not a tempo, not a loudness, not a speed in any audible sense. It is a *direction* — an arrow through this latent space, the 256-dimensional material of Chapter 2, pointing the way that lies toward music. A nudge, not a verdict. The model is not declaring what the finished piece is; it is only indicating which way lies *more like music* from the spot you currently occupy. Imagine a tuner who cannot tell you the right pitch outright but can always tell you "sharpen" or "flatten," and by how much, from wherever the string sits right now. Follow that guidance in enough small moves and you arrive in tune — without the tuner ever having named the target note. This is how you steer, too: you cannot tell the model "make it F#," but you can nudge the material and let it read the new situation and adapt.

The entire generation is the accumulation of these directional nudges. Step, read, nudge. Step, read, nudge. (The model nudges each time.) Each step moves the material a little way along the arrow the model just drew; then, from the new position, the model reads again and draws a fresh arrow. The material walks — from maximum chaos toward coherent, audible order — one informed nudge at a time.

This is what the field means by *flow matching*, or *rectified flow*. The names sound forbidding; the idea is homely. There is a path leading from noise to signal, and the model has been trained to be a trustworthy guide *at every point along that path* — to always know which way points toward music, whether the material is nearly pure static or nearly a finished song. Training is the work of making the guide reliable everywhere. Generation is the act of following it.

One thing about that reading you should carry forward. When the model "reads what is now there," it does not glance at each moment in isolation; the arrow drawn for any single slice is informed by the whole half-formed piece, which is why large-scale coherence can be carved out of noise at all. The guide does not merely smooth each instant — it hears the developing form. The mechanism that lets the moments listen to one another, and the reason musical structure actually lives there, is the whole of Chapter 5, "How Moments Listen."

## The walk from molten to set

There is a quantity that tracks where you are along this walk — how raw the material still is versus how finished. Think of the glassblower's gather: fresh from the furnace — fully molten — every shape is still possible; as it cools it firms, and the room for change narrows toward none. The walk runs from fully molten, at the start, to fully set, at the end. The model uses its sense of where it sits along that range to judge how to read the material and how boldly to nudge.

This chapter names that quantity and stops there, because it has a chapter of its own. It is called *sigma*, and the full story of what it governs — why the coarse architecture of a piece gets settled early while fine surface detail is left for last, and how that asymmetry becomes a control surface in its own right — is the subject of Chapter 4. For now, carry one image only: a single number that walks from one (molten) down toward zero (set), and that the whole carving process leans on to know how much latitude remains.

Sigma does not merely tell the model where it stands; it re-tunes the model itself as it drops. The model's own answer — which way is music — is continuously re-voiced as sigma cools, through adaptive layer normalisation (Chapter 6), so that the guidance grows more cautious and precise as the material firms. The arrow drawn at the first molten step and the arrow drawn near the set end are not the same instrument in the same voicing; they come from the same network re-voiced for the state of the material.

## Why a path is steerable and a pour is not

Here is the crux of the chapter, the reason it sits where it does in the book. *Because generation is a walk of many small steps rather than one decisive pour, it is open to steering at all.*

A pour gives you no purchase. The material goes in, the casting comes out; there is no moment in between where a hand could enter. But a walk is nothing but in-between moments. Between every step and the next there is a pause — the material sitting in a definite, readable, *modifiable* state, waiting for the next nudge. That pause is a doorway, and there are dozens of them in a single generation, strung along the path like frets along a neck. The iterative nature of the process is not an implementation detail or an efficiency concern; it is the very thing that makes the instrument playable. A model that leapt from noise to finished music in one jump would be magnificent and utterly mute to your hands. The steps are the handholds.

So where, concretely, can a hand go in? This chapter names two pressure points — the two that everything downstream is painted on.

**The first pressure point is the noisy material itself, mid-walk.** At any pause between steps, the half-carved latent is just sitting there, partly molten, in a known state. You can lean on it directly — shift it in a direction the model did *not* propose — and then let go. The model takes its next reading from the *moved* material, not the material it expected, and carries on from this new reality. You changed the situation; the guide adapts to what it now finds. Like reaching in and nudging the still-spinning clay between the potter's own passes: you do not fight the potter, you alter what their next pass meets.

**The second pressure point is subtler, and you must own it fully now**, because a whole later technique rests on your already understanding it. At every single step, in order to decide which way to nudge, the model forms a *running guess at the finished piece*. From wherever the material currently sits — even early, even when it is still mostly static — the model projects: *if I followed my own guidance all the way to the end from here, this is roughly what the settled latent would be.* It is a provisional vision of the destination, recomputed fresh at every step, sharpening as the walk proceeds. Think of the woodworker who, with the joint only half cut, holds the two pieces loosely together and *sees* the finished fit — adjusting the next paring to that imagined whole. Early in the walk this guess is vague, dreamlike. Late in the walk it is nearly the real thing. But it is *always present*, the model's current best picture of where all this is heading.

And that guess is a second surface you can press on. Instead of shoving the raw, noisy material around, you can lean on the model's *imagined finished piece* — push that vision toward what you want — and let the corrected vision inform the very next nudge. Pressing the molten clay and adjusting the woodworker's held-up preview of the joint are different gestures with different leverage, even though both, in the end, bend the same walk. That they differ — when each bites hardest, what each is good for — is the craft Chapter 13 teaches. This chapter's job is only to make sure you can *see* both doorways: the material as it is, and the destination as the model currently imagines it.

Some of those leans will not be a single shove but a loop: measure how far the material is from a target you have set, nudge accordingly, read again, measure again. That closed loop, rather than an open one, is the subject of Chapter 13.

## Many small steps, many small openings

Step back and take in the shape of the whole. A pour is a single event with no interior. A walk is a corridor lined with doors. Every step the model takes is a moment when the material rests in a definite state, ready to be read — and so every step is a moment when *you* could read it too, and lean. Some of those leans will go with the grain of the material, strengthening the direction the model already favours. Some will cut across the grain, asking for something the model would not have reached on its own, and the carving will resist, or comply roughly, or surprise you. Some will hold part of the material fixed throughout the walk — a technique called *inpainting*, where you freeze regions the model must carve around as if they were unmovable stone (Chapter 10). Learning the difference from the outside — by ear, by patient auditioning — is much of what it means to become a player of this instrument rather than merely an operator of it.

The carver's hands can enter at either of those two pressure points — the raw material or the model's running vision of the finished piece — at any pass in the walk. That is the whole gift of an iterative process, and it is the canvas under everything that follows. Those handholds become even more powerful when paired with measurement (Chapter 13): a head that reads the latent and reports back a single musical feature, so you can nudge not blindly but toward a target.

## Openings

- Which features of the music get roughed out in the earliest passes, while the material is still nearly static — tempo, energy, the broad arc — and which are left for the final cuts? Is there a coarse-to-fine order to the carving, and if so, does it line up with the molten-to-set walk Chapter 4 takes up? (That *some* coarse-to-fine order exists is known; whether it maps cleanly onto the things a musician would name is open.)
- Does the *character* of a step change as the material settles? Is the model's sense of "which way is music" a confident, sweeping arrow early on and a cautious, local one near the end — and how would you even tell from the outside? (speculative)
- The running guess of the finished piece sharpens as the walk proceeds. Is there a point along the path where it becomes trustworthy enough to steer against — and pressing on a vague early guess versus a near-final one, are those the same act or two different tools? (frontier)
- What does it mean to steer *with* the grain of the material versus *against* it? When you lean in a direction the model already favoured, you strengthen what it wanted; when you cut across, it resists. How would you hear that resistance, and what does it tell you about the dimension you are trying to control? (speculative)
- If every pause between steps is a doorway, are some doorways wider than others — does a nudge applied early in the walk reach further into the final result than the same nudge applied late, and which of the two pressure points is more sensitive to *when* you press it? (frontier)
- If you lean on both pressure points at once — nudging the raw material *and* the projected clean vision — what happens when they pull in different directions, and can you use that tension deliberately? (frontier)
