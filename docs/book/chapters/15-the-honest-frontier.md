# Chapter 15 - The Honest Frontier: Per-Moment Molten-ness and Live Feedback

Every instrument has a frontier where the builder's knowledge gives way to intuition, and beyond that to imagination — and the good luthier always knows which of the three lands they stand in: *this joint I have measured a hundred times; this voicing I am guessing at; this idea I simply hope is true.* That sorting is not a weakness in the craft; it is the craft.

This chapter is about the frontier of Stable Audio 3 — two specific places where the instrument is genuinely unfinished. But more than either, it is about the sorting itself: holding what you *know*, what you *speculate*, and what you merely *hope* in three separate hands and never letting them blur. That habit is the most portable tool in this book. It lets you judge a claim — including the claims this book makes — by where it sits, not by how exciting it sounds.

## The seam we have been circling

Since Chapter Four you have lived with a quiet asterisk. Sigma — the molten-ness number, the single dial that says how much the material can still be moved — is *one value for the whole clip*. At every step of the carve, the downbeat of bar one and the dying chord at the very end are equally molten, or equally set. The instrument has one furnace temperature for the entire gather of glass.

Here is the first thing to get exactly right, because the whole chapter pivots on it. That single-sigma design is **not a law of diffusion**. Nothing in the physics of carving order from noise requires every moment to share a temperature. It is a convenience — a simplification baked into how this instrument was built and trained. The model learned to denoise under the assumption that the whole clip is always at one molten-ness, and so that assumption is woven into its reflexes. But the assumption and the mathematics underneath it are two different things, and the gap between them is exactly where a builder's hand can go.

Lift that constraint and sigma stops being a dial. It becomes a **field** — one molten-ness per slice, a surface stretched across the clip that you could shape the way a glassblower shapes a gather: the bowl set firm while the lip stays soft enough to work, the heat varying smoothly from region to region. This is a new control surface, unlike any other in the book. The prompt steers *what*. CFG steers *how hard*. A control head steers *one measured quantity*. A sigma field would steer *which parts of time are still negotiable* — independently, moment by moment.

Set this beside the masking you met in Chapter Ten, because the two are easy to confuse and the difference is the whole point. **Masking holds moments fixed in time** — you freeze a region (these bars stay exactly as they are) and let the rest regenerate. **A sigma field holds moments fixed in molten-ness** — you decide not *which bars are finished* but *how finished each is allowed to be* at this step. They are orthogonal axes: masking is a binary clamp on the timeline, the sigma field a continuous temperature across it. That they do not collide — you could run both at once — is exactly what tells you the sigma field is a genuinely new control, not a re-dressing of masking.

## Where the field would take you

Picture the obvious application and you will see why people want this. Hold the slices near the end of your clip at high sigma — still molten, still open — while the slices you have already heard have cooled to near zero, fully committed. Now slide that arrangement forward in time: as the molten edge sets, expose fresh molten material beyond it. The music never stops being generated, and never has to stop being *heard*. This is the mechanical basis of what researchers call **streaming** or **endless** generation. You never lift the bow off the string; the string simply stays in motion, the played portion firm behind the contact point, the unplayed portion still liquid ahead.

Endless generation is the headline use, but not the only thing a sigma field would buy you. You could **cool slowly in a region you intend to work hard with a control head** — keep a passage molten longer so a guidance head has many steps to shape its bassline or brightness — while the surrounding music sets quickly. Or hold a *single instant* — a transition, a drop, a fill — molten far longer than its neighbours, lavishing detail on the moment that carries the most weight. Once molten-ness is a surface you draw rather than a dial you set, "where do I spend my carving effort?" becomes a question you answer per region instead of per clip.

Say plainly where this stands, because the temptation to oversell is enormous. **This is real, and it is partial.** A field of per-moment sigma is not a fantasy — partial implementations exist in the wider research world, and there are early, exploratory experiments in the very repository you have been working in. The surgery lives in `stable_audio_3/inference/fifo_infinite.py` on branch `latch-sa3-phase1`: a guarded patch making the timestep carry a value *per slice* rather than one value for the whole clip. But "experiments in the repository" must be read with a cold eye. The honest label here is *code written, untested*. Someone has performed the surgery; nobody has yet shown, with audio you would actually want to hear, that the instrument plays well that way. Written is not working.

Notice how sharply this differs from its respectable, shipping cousin. In Chapter Ten you met the sliding window — long-form rendering by inching a mask forward, freezing what you have, exposing a little more future, repeating. That **works**; you can use it today. But it achieves continuity by *re-running the global carve on each new window*, holding the past fixed through inpainting — the whole clip inside each window still shares one sigma. Per-moment sigma is the deeper cousin that would dissolve the window entirely: no discrete hops, no seams to hide, a single continuous melt travelling the length of the piece. The sliding window pours a series of overlapping buckets; the sigma field would *be* the river. One ships. One is a frontier.

## The tuner that never stops listening

The second frontier lives in Chapter Thirteen's territory, pushed to a place that chapter deliberately did not go. There, a control head was a tuning peg: you set a target, and at certain steps the head read the latent, measured the gap, and nudged — an occasional prod, a tuner you hold up to the string now and then, check the pitch, set down. Now imagine the tuner that *never sets down* — listening at every carving step, keeping its hand lightly on the material the whole way, correcting continuously rather than at intervals. Not check-and-release but a closed loop woven *through* the sampling process rather than bolted *alongside* it: the difference between a singer who glances at a tuning meter between phrases and one who lives inside a feedback monitor the whole time, voice and reference fused into a single ongoing act of intonation.

It is a compelling picture, and **largely unproven** at the scale and resolution that would make it genuinely musical. The reasons it might *not* work are worth reasoning through, because they teach you how to interrogate any frontier claim. A loop that corrects at every step can also *oscillate* — over-correct, then over-correct the over-correction, the way a poorly damped feedback circuit howls.

What does that oscillation actually *sound* like, though? A musician needs this answered, because "the loop oscillates" is an engineer's phrase, not a listener's. The signature depends on the timescale of the wobble. A slow oscillation — the loop hunting back and forth across several carving steps — would surface as **instability in the steered quantity itself**: a bassline that swells and ducks without reason, a brightness that breathes in and out, a loudness that pumps. A faster oscillation, fighting the model's own step-to-step momentum, would read as **texture damage** — graininess, smearing, a loss of clean attack on notes, the audio equivalent of a surface chattering under a tool pushed too hard. In the worst case it is a meter satisfied while the music curdles: the steered number sits perfectly on target and the ear hears something hollow, over-processed, wrong in a way it cannot name. None of these are reasons it *cannot* work. They are the first things that would go wrong, and naming them in advance — *this is what failure would taste like* — is how you design the experiment that settles it.

A quieter, mechanical hazard hides inside this one, connecting straight back to Chapter Thirteen's hardest lesson: a guidance nudge must be **scaled to match the latent's own magnitude** — too weak and the head is a mirage, too strong and it overruns the music. For a head that fires once or twice, you find one good gain and you are done. For a head firing at *every* step, the right gain may not hold still: the material's scale differs when molten versus nearly set, so a nudge gentle early may be violent late, or the reverse. Predict this gotcha and you save yourself a baffling afternoon — the continuous head that behaves beautifully through the first half of the carve and then either fades into irrelevance or tears the texture apart as sigma drops. The fix is almost certainly a gain that *tracks* sigma rather than a constant, but nobody has tuned that schedule cleanly enough to hand you the curve. That, too, is the frontier.

## The discipline: blocked by what?

Here is the meta-skill the whole chapter is built to hand you, more valuable than either frontier above. Meeting a frontier idea, learn to ask one question before any other: **is this blocked by something fundamental, or merely by engineering effort?**

The two feel identical from outside — in both cases the thing does not yet work — but they could not be more different to a builder. *Blocked by engineering* means the path is known and someone simply has to walk it: write the code, run the trials, tune the gains, spend the compute. *Blocked by something fundamental* means a reason rooted in how the instrument actually works that no effort dissolves — an assumption learned so deeply that violating it breaks the thing you were trying to use.

Per-moment sigma sits, beautifully, right on this fault line. The surgery to make the timestep carry one value per slice is *engineering* — finite, doable, partly done. But the model was *trained* on a single global molten-ness. Hand it a clip half-set and half-molten at the same instant, and you ask it to behave in a configuration it has never once seen. If its learned reflexes generalise, the remaining work is engineering. If they do not — if its whole sense of "how molten is this material" assumes uniformity so deeply that mixed states produce incoherence — then the block is *fundamental*, and the honest fix is not more code but *retraining the instrument to expect a field*.

So which side is it on? The discipline is not just to ask but to design the experiment that answers, and per-moment sigma is a clean case to learn on. You would not build the whole streaming machine first. You would build the smallest probe that could *only* tell you whether the model tolerates a non-uniform sigma at all — for example:

- **The two-region probe.** Split a clip into two halves carved at *different* molten-nesses. Listen at the boundary: does the music stay one piece, or does a seam open?
- **The gentle-gradient probe.** Instead of a sharp split, impose a smooth ramp of sigma — barely non-uniform. If even a gentle gradient produces incoherence, the constraint is deep. If gentle works and sharp fails, you have *located* the breaking point, not merely confirmed it.
- **The hold-one-instant probe.** Keep the clip uniform except for a single slice held molten a little longer — the most surgical violation possible. If even one slice cannot disagree with its neighbours, the assumption is woven in at the finest grain.
- **The schedule-skew probe.** Keep sigma uniform in *space* but let regions reach a given molten-ness at different *steps*, separating "can it hold a spatial gradient?" from "can it hold a temporal one?"

The point is not the list but the *shape* of the move: facing any frontier, you do not bet a month on the full prototype. You enumerate the smallest experiments that each rule out one way the thing could be fundamentally blocked, run the cheapest first, and let the answer decide whether to keep walking. That same ladder of minimal probes is how you would interrogate the continuous-feedback frontier, or any frontier this book has not imagined.

And the diagnosis changes what you *build first*. Believe a block is engineering, and you build the prototype. Suspect it is fundamental, and you build the probe instead, and let it decide everything downstream. One reassurance belongs here: not knowing which side a frontier falls on is the **normal** condition of real building, not a sign you skipped your homework. The luthier carving a new bracing pattern does not know whether it will sing or wolf until the box is closed and bowed; that uncertainty is not ignorance to be ashamed of but the exact thing the experiment exists to resolve. Evidence first, architecture second, excitement third — and the excitement is real, and allowed; it simply comes last.

## What holds a half-set gather together?

Which leaves the deepest open question of all — the one I want to leave in your hands rather than answer. If a passage near the end of the clip is still molten while the passage you are hearing has set hard, *what holds the boundary between them coherent?* What stops the firm music and the liquid music from belonging to two different pieces — a tempo that drifts across the seam, a key that does not survive the transition, a groove that locks on one side and dissolves on the other?

You already know what has to do the holding, because you met it in Chapter Five. **Attention** — every moment listening to every other moment — is the only mechanism in this instrument positioned to reach across the molten-to-set boundary and keep both sides speaking the same musical language. It is the load-bearing force here: firm bars and liquid bars cohere only if the listening that builds form *survives* a clip at many molten-nesses at once. And riding inside that listening is the model's sense of relative time — RoPE, from Chapter Seven, which tells each slice it sits *N* steps after another, learned on clips of *uniform* molten-ness. If regions now linger at wildly different temperatures, does the relative timeline still read cleanly, or does attention's grip on *when* loosen exactly where molten and set meet? A mechanical question, and genuinely open.

But not *groundless*, and this should encourage you. We already have a partial observation in the shipping sliding window: it stitches independently-carved blocks at seams and — when working — stays coherent across them, tempo and key and groove surviving the join, because attention reaches across the overlap. So attention's grip is strong enough to hold a deliberate boundary together — under the easier condition that both sides are fully set. The open question sharpens: does that same grip survive when one side is not set but still molten? The sliding window proves the reach exists across a difference in *content*; the frontier asks whether it holds across a difference in *temperature*.

You already know the acoustic analogue, because you have felt it: the thermal stress at the line in a glass gather where hot meets cool too sharply, and the piece cracks as it cools. Whether attention is the annealing that lets hot and cool meet without cracking — whether it reaches across a deliberate sigma discontinuity the way it reaches across a content seam — may be the single load-bearing question for this entire frontier. Sit with it.

## Openings

- If sigma became a field rather than a single value, what would you use to *sculpt* it — a curve you draw by hand, a function of musical position, a signal driven by a control head — and how would you describe the shape you want to someone who could build it?
- A control head as a continuous voice rather than an occasional prod: what would tell you it is *listening* rather than *forcing*? Now that you know oscillation can read as a pumping of the steered quantity or a chattering of the texture, which would your ear catch first?
- The global-sigma constraint turned out to be a convenience, not a law. What *other* assumptions in this instrument — quietly accepted as fixed since Chapter One — might also be conveniences waiting to be lifted, and which do you suspect are genuinely load-bearing?
- Take any frontier claim, including the ones in this chapter, and place it: blocked by engineering, or by something fundamental? What is the smallest probe on the ladder that would move it from one category to the other?
- The sliding window proves attention can hold a boundary when both sides are set. What would you have to see at the seam to believe it also holds when one side is still molten — and what is the analogue, in an instrument you play, of a join that survives a difference in *temperature* rather than just *material*?
