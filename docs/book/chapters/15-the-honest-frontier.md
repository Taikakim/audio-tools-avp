# Chapter 15 - The Honest Frontier: Per-Moment Molten-ness and Live Feedback

Every instrument has a frontier where the builder's knowledge gives way to intuition, and beyond that to imagination — and the good luthier always knows which of the three lands they stand in: *this joint I have measured a hundred times; this voicing I am guessing at; this idea I simply hope is true.* That sorting is not a weakness in the craft; it is the craft.

This chapter is about the frontier of Stable Audio 3 — two places where the instrument is genuinely unfinished. But more than either, it is about that sorting: holding the *known*, the *speculative*, and the merely *hoped* in three separate hands. The habit lets you judge a claim — including the claims this book makes — by where it sits, not by how exciting it sounds.

## The seam we have been circling

Since Chapter Four you have lived with a quiet asterisk. Sigma — the molten-ness number, the single dial for how much the material can still be moved — is *one value for the whole clip*. At every step of the carve, the downbeat of bar one and the dying chord at the very end are equally molten, the way a single damper pedal lifts every string in the piano at once: you cannot leave the bass ringing while you mute the treble.

That single-sigma design is **not a law of diffusion**. Nothing in the physics of carving order from noise requires every moment to share a temperature; it is a convenience baked into how this instrument was trained. The model learned to denoise assuming the whole clip is always at one molten-ness, so that assumption is woven into its reflexes — but a learned reflex is not the underlying mathematics, and that gap is where a builder's hand can go.

Lift that constraint and sigma stops being a dial. It becomes a **field** — one molten-ness per slice. Think of the stops on a pipe organ, each rank drawn or pushed in independently: a separate handle for every region of time, this passage held open while that one is locked shut. The prompt steers *what*. CFG steers *how hard*. A control head steers *one measured quantity*. A sigma field would steer *which parts of time are still negotiable* — moment by moment.

Set this beside the masking you met in Chapter Ten, easy to confuse and the difference is the whole point. **Masking holds moments fixed in time** — you freeze a region and let the rest regenerate. **A sigma field holds moments fixed in molten-ness** — you decide not *which bars are finished* but *how finished each is allowed to be* at this step. They are orthogonal: masking is a binary clamp on the timeline, the sigma field a continuous temperature across it. That they do not collide — you could run both at once — is what tells you the sigma field is genuinely new, not a re-dressing of masking.

## Where the field would take you

Picture the obvious application. Hold the slices near the end of your clip molten while the slices you have already heard cool to near zero; then slide that arrangement forward, and as the molten edge sets, expose fresh molten material beyond it. The music never stops being generated, and never has to stop being *heard* — what researchers call **streaming** or **endless** generation. You never lift the bow off the string; the played portion stays firm behind the contact point, the unplayed portion still liquid ahead.

Endless generation is the headline use, but not the only thing a sigma field would buy you. You could **cool slowly in a region you intend to work hard with a control head** — keep a passage molten longer so a guidance head has many steps to shape its bassline or brightness — while the surrounding music sets quickly. Or hold a *single instant* — a transition, a drop, a fill — molten far longer than its neighbours, lavishing detail on the moment that carries the most weight. "Where do I spend my carving effort?" becomes a question answered per region instead of per clip.

**This is real, and it is partial.** Partial implementations exist in the wider research world, and there are early, exploratory experiments in the very repository you have been working in. The surgery lives in `stable_audio_3/inference/fifo_infinite.py` on branch `latch-sa3-phase1`: a guarded patch making the timestep carry a value *per slice* rather than one value for the whole clip. But the honest label is *code written, untested* — nobody has yet shown, with audio you would want to hear, that the instrument plays well that way. Written is not working.

Notice how sharply this differs from its respectable, shipping cousin. In Chapter Ten you met the sliding window — long-form rendering by inching a mask forward, freezing what you have, exposing a little more future, repeating. That **works**; you can use it today. But it achieves continuity by *re-running the global carve on each new window*, holding the past fixed through inpainting — the clip inside each window still shares one sigma. Per-moment sigma would dissolve the window entirely: no discrete hops, a single continuous melt travelling the length of the piece. The sliding window pours a series of overlapping buckets; the sigma field would *be* the river. One ships; one is a frontier.

## The tuner that never stops listening

The second frontier lives in Chapter Thirteen's territory, pushed where that chapter deliberately did not go. There, a control head was a tuning peg: at certain steps it read the latent, measured the gap, and nudged — a tuner you hold up to the string now and then, check, set down. Now imagine the tuner that *never sets down*: a closed loop woven *through* the sampling process rather than bolted *alongside* it, correcting at every carving step. The difference between a singer who glances at a tuning meter between phrases and one who lives inside a feedback monitor the whole time.

It is a compelling picture, and **largely unproven** at the scale and resolution that would make it genuinely musical. Reasoning through why it might *not* work teaches you how to interrogate any frontier claim. A loop correcting at every step can *oscillate* — over-correct, then over-correct the over-correction, the way a poorly damped circuit howls.

But what does that oscillation *sound* like? "The loop oscillates" is an engineer's phrase, not a listener's, and the signature depends on the timescale of the wobble. A slow oscillation — the loop hunting back and forth across several carving steps — would surface as **instability in the steered quantity itself**: a bassline that swells and ducks without reason, a brightness that breathes, a loudness that pumps. A faster one, fighting the model's own step-to-step momentum, would read as **texture damage** — graininess, smearing, a loss of clean attack, a surface chattering under a tool pushed too hard. In the worst case the meter is satisfied while the music curdles: the steered number sits on target and the ear hears something hollow, over-processed, wrong in a way it cannot name. None of these mean it *cannot* work; they are the first things that would go wrong, and naming them is how you design the experiment that settles it.

A quieter, mechanical hazard hides inside this one, running straight back to the hardest lesson of Chapter Thirteen — that a guidance nudge must be **scaled to match the latent's own magnitude**, too weak and the head is a mirage, too strong and it overruns the music. For a head that fires once or twice, you find one good gain and you are done. For a head firing at *every* step, the right gain may not hold still: the material's scale differs when molten versus nearly set, so a nudge gentle early may be violent late. The continuous head behaves beautifully through the first half of the carve, then either fades into irrelevance or tears the texture apart as sigma drops. The fix is almost certainly a gain that *tracks* sigma rather than a constant, but nobody has tuned that schedule cleanly enough to hand you the curve. That, too, is the frontier.

## The discipline: blocked by what?

This is the meta-skill of the chapter, more valuable than either frontier above. Meeting any frontier idea, ask one question first: **is this blocked by something fundamental, or merely by engineering effort?**

The two feel identical from outside — the thing does not yet work — but they could not be more different to a builder. *Blocked by engineering* means the path is known and someone simply has to walk it: write the code, run the trials, tune the gains, spend the compute. *Blocked by something fundamental* means a reason rooted in how the instrument works that no effort dissolves — an assumption learned so deeply that violating it breaks the thing you were trying to use.

Per-moment sigma sits right on this fault line. The surgery to make the timestep carry one value per slice is *engineering* — finite, doable, partly done. But the model was *trained* on a single global molten-ness. Hand it a clip half-set and half-molten at the same instant, and you ask it to behave in a configuration it has never once seen. If its learned reflexes generalise, the remaining work is engineering. If they do not — if its whole sense of "how molten is this material" assumes uniformity so deeply that mixed states produce incoherence — then the block is *fundamental*, and the honest fix is not more code but *retraining the instrument to expect a field*.

So which side is it on? The discipline is not just to ask but to design the experiment that answers. You would not build the whole streaming machine first; you would build the smallest probe that could *only* tell you whether the model tolerates a non-uniform sigma:

- **The two-region probe.** Split a clip into two halves carved at *different* molten-nesses. Listen at the boundary: does the music stay one piece, or does a seam open?
- **The gentle-gradient probe.** Instead of a sharp split, impose a smooth ramp of sigma. If even a gentle gradient produces incoherence, the constraint is deep. If gentle works and sharp fails, you have *located* the breaking point, not merely confirmed it.
- **The hold-one-instant probe.** Keep the clip uniform except for a single slice held molten a little longer — the most surgical violation possible. If even one slice cannot disagree with its neighbours, the assumption is woven in at the finest grain.
- **The schedule-skew probe.** Keep sigma uniform in *space* but let regions reach a given molten-ness at different *steps*, separating "can it hold a spatial gradient?" from "can it hold a temporal one?"

The point is not the list but the *shape* of the move: enumerate the smallest experiments that each rule out one way the thing could be fundamentally blocked, run the cheapest first, and let the answer decide whether to keep walking. That same ladder is how you would interrogate the continuous-feedback frontier, or any frontier this book has not imagined — believe a block is engineering and you build the prototype; suspect it is fundamental and you build the probe.

Not knowing which side a frontier falls on is the **normal** condition of real building, not a sign you skipped your homework: the luthier carving a new bracing pattern does not know whether it will sing or wolf until the box is closed and bowed. That uncertainty is what the experiment exists to resolve. Evidence first, architecture second, excitement third — and the excitement is real and allowed; it simply comes last.

## What holds a half-set gather together?

Which leaves the deepest open question, the one I want to leave in your hands rather than answer. If a passage near the end of the clip is still molten while the passage you are hearing has set hard, *what holds the boundary between them coherent?* What stops the firm music and the liquid music from belonging to two different pieces — a tempo that drifts across the seam, a key that does not survive the transition, a groove that locks on one side and dissolves on the other?

You already know what has to do the holding, because you met it in Chapter Five. **Attention** — every moment listening to every other moment — is the only mechanism in this instrument positioned to reach across the molten-to-set boundary and keep both sides speaking the same musical language; firm bars and liquid bars cohere only if that listening *survives* a clip at many molten-nesses at once. And riding inside it is the model's sense of relative time — RoPE, from Chapter Seven, which tells each slice it sits *N* steps after another, learned on clips of *uniform* molten-ness. If regions now linger at wildly different temperatures, does the relative timeline still read cleanly, or does attention's grip on *when* loosen exactly where molten and set meet? A mechanical question, and genuinely open.

But not *groundless*. The shipping sliding window already gives a partial observation: it stitches independently-carved blocks at seams and — when working — stays coherent across them, tempo and key and groove surviving the join, because attention reaches across the overlap. So the grip holds a deliberate boundary together when both sides are fully set: the reach exists across a difference in *content*. The frontier asks whether it also holds across a difference in *temperature*.

You already know the analogue, because you have felt it: a bell cast with cool metal poured against hot cracks along the seam as it sets, the join failing where two temperatures met too sharply. Whether attention is the slow, even cooling that lets hot and cool meet without cracking may be the single load-bearing question for this entire frontier. Sit with it.

## Openings

- *(frontier)* If sigma became a field rather than a single value, what would you use to *sculpt* it — a curve you draw by hand, a function of musical position, a signal driven by a control head — and how would you describe the shape you want to someone who could build it?
- *(frontier)* A control head as a continuous voice rather than an occasional prod: what would tell you it is *listening* rather than *forcing*? Now that you know oscillation can read as a pumping of the steered quantity or a chattering of the texture, which would your ear catch first — and would the first sign even be audible, or only visible on a meter?
- *(speculative)* The global-sigma constraint turned out to be a convenience, not a law. What *other* assumptions in this instrument — quietly accepted as fixed since Chapter One — might also be conveniences waiting to be lifted, and which do you suspect are genuinely load-bearing?
- *(known)* Take any frontier claim, including the ones in this chapter, and place it: blocked by engineering, or by something fundamental? What is the smallest probe on the ladder that would move it from one category to the other?
- *(frontier)* The sliding window proves attention can hold a boundary when both sides are set. What would you have to see at the seam to believe it also holds when one side is still molten — and what is the analogue, in an instrument you play, of a join that survives a difference in *temperature* rather than just *material*?
