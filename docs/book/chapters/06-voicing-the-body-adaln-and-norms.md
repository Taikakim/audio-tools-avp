# Chapter 6 - Voicing the Whole Body: adaLN and the Stabilisers

A luthier learns early that voicing is never a single act, finished and signed. You graduate the top, you shave the bracing, you tap and listen — but the instrument's response is not fixed by those cuts alone. Press a string harder and the body answers differently. Warm the room and the wood loosens; chill it and the top stiffens, the highs come forward, the bass recedes. The instrument has a body, but how that body *responds* shifts continuously with the conditions around it. Voicing is less a destination than a living relationship between the wood and the moment.

The carver at the heart of SA3 — the generative model that walks noise toward music — works on a strikingly similar principle. And the thing playing the role of "the room's temperature," the condition that re-voices the whole body moment to moment, is sigma itself: the molten-ness number you met in Chapter 4. That chapter left you with a question — whether it is wise that a single furnace heats the whole gather of glass, one molten-ness shared by every moment of the clip. This chapter shows you the machinery that furnace actually drives. We will not re-explain sigma; we will trace where it *goes* once it has a value.

## The Three Global Knobs

The mechanism that lets sigma re-voice the body is called adaptive layer normalisation, adaLN for short. Strip away the name and here is the picture: wired into every block of the carver — and there are many, stacked deep — sits a small set of three knobs. A *scale*, a *shift*, and a *gate*.

Borrow the language of the mixing console. The scale is a channel gain — how loudly a layer's internal voice speaks. The shift is a bias, a DC offset sliding the whole signal up or down, changing what counts as centred. The gate is the fader at the channel's output — how much of what this block computed actually passes to the next, from "let it all through" to "hold this block back entirely." None of these act on audio; they act on the internal state of each layer as it works — the network's private workspace, where it turns noise into the shape of music.

Now the crucial part. Those three knobs are not set once and bolted down. They are recomputed *fresh at every step of the carve*, and their settings flow from a single object you should learn to name: the **global conditioning vector**. This is an *input*, assembled new each run, and it already carries several things fused together — the current sigma (how molten is the material right now?), the intended duration in seconds, and a compact summary of the prompt. One bundle of numbers that the modulation machinery knows how to read. Feed it in, and out come the scale, shift, and gate for every block.

Make this concrete. Probe one block early in the carve, at sigma near 0.8, the material still mostly noise: the modulation might hand it a gate near 0.3 — *hold most of this back, too molten for fine work* — and keep its scale modest. Run the same block late, at sigma near 0.2, the music nearly set, and the same machinery might open that gate toward 0.9 and lift the scale — *speak fully now; there is real structure to refine.* Same block, same weights, two completely different voicings, and all that changed was the molten-ness folded into the global vector. (Treat those numbers as a sketch to think with, not measurements; the point is the *direction* of the swing, not the digits.)

So as generation walks from molten toward set, the whole deep stack is continuously re-voiced — early, when the material is fluid, attending to bones; late, near the finish, to surface. It is as if your guitar re-graduated its own bracing depending on how hard you were leaning into the note — stiffening for the broad strokes, loosening for the fine. The body does not change. Its *responsiveness* changes, everywhere at once, on a schedule driven by the molten-ness of the moment.

## The Hook Hiding in Plain Sight

Recall the carver's other door from Chapter 5: cross-attention, where each block *reaches for* the translated words and reads as much detail as it needs. Hold the contrast in one line — cross-attention is information a block *reaches for*; the global vector is a condition the block is *immersed in*, applied to every layer whether it asks or not. Players reading their own charts, versus the temperature of the hall that no player consults yet every instrument obeys.

Here is what should make a builder sit up. The global conditioning vector is an *input* — not an emergent property buried out of reach, but a real, locatable signal that gets assembled and fed to the modulation. And it already carries more than one thing: sigma, duration (Chapter 8's to own — note only that it proves the vector is a place where *measured quantities* get folded in), and the prompt's summary.

Which raises the obvious, generative question: what *else* could ride in it?

Anything you could express as a vector and fuse into that signal would re-voice not one layer, not one head, but the entire body at once. Mark this carefully, because it is a *third category* of control, distinct from the two this book keeps circling. A LoRA changes the instrument's body at training time and persists into every future performance. A control head adds a sense and a feedback loop, pressing on the *material* — the latent — during one carve. The global conditioning vector is neither: it is **control-at-runtime that re-voices the whole body without any training-time cost**, a lever already wired to reach every block, free at each performance, touching no weights. One adjusts the clay; another retunes the body permanently; this one adjusts *the hands*, fresh each run.

Whether you fold a new signal in at training time — teaching the network from the start to read it — or at inference time, reaching into the assembled vector before it drives the modulation, is exactly where the design questions live. The two are not equivalent: a network never taught to read a channel may simply ignore it, or worse, mistake it for noise it has learned to suppress. We leave that as an opening, not a recipe — the book's job here is to hand you the handle, not to pretend the door is mapped.

## The Quiet Bracing

Beside this live, expressive voicing sits a second family of mechanisms, and they could not be less glamorous. They have names like QK-normalisation, RMS-like norms, dynamic-tanh, running statistics. You will never reach for them as a control. But they are the reason any control works at all.

Return to the workshop. A great soundboard is not just thin and resonant; it is *braced* — graduated struts that let it flex where flexing makes tone and stiffen it where stiffness prevents collapse. A bell that rings too freely develops a wolf-tone: one note that howls, swamps its neighbours, makes the instrument unplayable in that region. The luthier's bracing and damping are not there to make the instrument *expressive*. They keep its expressiveness from tearing it apart.

The stabilisers play exactly this role inside the carver. Attention, left unbraced, is prone to its own wolf-tone: a few values grow enormous, dominate everything, and the whole computation howls into one screaming note that drowns the music. QK-normalisation is the brace that keeps attention's internal levels in range so no single comparison runs away with the room. The RMS-like norms and running statistics keep signal levels through the deep stack from exploding toward infinity or collapsing toward silence as they pass block after block — damping that keeps a long, resonant body from accumulating a rattle over its length. Dynamic-tanh and its kin are soft limiters that fold back the extremes before they turn destructive, the way a well-voiced top refuses to let one frequency take over.

None of this is the voice. All of it is the bracing that lets the voice exist.

## Stability Is the Precondition of Expressiveness

Here is the conceptual gift this chapter exists to give you, and it is worth stating plainly because the rest of the book will spend it: **stability is not the opposite of expressiveness. It is its precondition.**

It is tempting to imagine the stabilisers as killjoys — constraints you would rip out, if only you dared, to let the instrument sing louder. The workshop knows better. An unbraced soundboard does not sing louder; it caves in. The bracing is what makes loud, free playing *survivable*: remove it and you get a rattle, a wolf-tone, a collapse — never more music.

This matters the moment you push on the instrument with your own controls — and it is why the stabilisers belong in a chapter you might have expected to be purely about voicing. When you drive a control head's nudge hard — really lean on the latent to bend it toward your target — you are testing how much the body can take before the bracing becomes the binding constraint. **There is a ceiling, and it is the single most important fact to carry into your control work.** Push gently and the network absorbs your nudge and stays coherent; the music bends your way. Push past the ceiling and the stabilisers can no longer hold the levels in range: your nudge stops *steering* and starts *clipping* — folding the material toward silence at one extreme or into incoherent mush at the other. The latent no longer decodes to music; it decodes to a rattle.

And the ceiling is not a fixed height. It almost certainly *moves with sigma*. When the material is molten — high sigma, the network's own internal signals already noisier and closer to their limiters — there is less headroom left before something saturates, so the ceiling sits lower. As the material sets toward low sigma, the levels calm and more headroom may open. (Whether it cleanly rises as the glass cools or dips somewhere mid-walk is exactly the kind of thing to go *measure* rather than take on faith.) This is why a single fixed nudge strength rarely behaves the same across the whole carve — and it foreshadows the per-band gain work Chapter 13 has to do.

Recognising the ceiling as a *principle* is this chapter's contribution. How high it sits, how to find it, how it shifts as the material sets — those are the calibration questions Chapter 13 cashes in when it tunes a head's gain to the latent's scale. The reason gain must be matched so carefully, the reason an over-strong nudge blows the material apart rather than steering it further, is precisely this bracing and its ceiling. The stabilisers *define the room* in which all control happens.

## Global Voicing, Local Detail

One last tension, because it is honest and it opens forward. AdaLN re-voices the whole body *at once*: a single sigma, a single global vector, every block modulated together. But the blocks are not all doing the same work — earlier ones tend toward coarse structure, later toward fine detail. Is one uniform whole-body re-voicing always what you want? Or is there something to learn where one block might be made *less* sensitive to the global signal than another?

Call it a selective deafness — like reaching into a conducted ensemble and asking one player to ignore the conductor's tempo while everyone else follows it, holding their part steady against the swing of the whole. The thought experiment is suggestive: if late-stage detail blocks were made partly deaf to sigma's "we're nearly done, refine the surface" instruction, might they keep doing structural work later into the carve than the global schedule intends — a local override of the global hand? We do not know. That is the honest answer, and exactly the kind of unvisited square the closing chapters are built to help you find.

There is a deeper, more dangerous assumption hiding here too, and it is fair to name now. The adaLN machinery has only ever been taught to read sigma as a single number that *descends monotonically* — molten to set, one direction, one value shared by the entire clip. Every voicing it has learned is keyed to that orderly walk; this is baked into the modulation, not a soft preference. So when later chapters reach for the frontier — a *per-moment* sigma, where different slices sit at different temperatures at once, the basis for endless and streaming generation — the break is not cosmetic. You would feed the global vector a situation it has never seen and was never built to voice: a body asked to be molten and set in the same breath. Whether the existing bracing even holds under that, let alone voices it well, is a genuinely open architectural question, not a tuning detail. Chapter 15 picks it up.

## Openings

- The global conditioning vector already reaches every block at once, already carrying sigma, duration, and a summary of the prompt. What would it mean to fold a *new* signal into it — and how is doing so at training time (teaching the body to read it from the start) fundamentally different from reaching into the assembled vector at inference time, where the network may simply ignore a channel it was never taught to hear?

- The stabilisers set a ceiling on how hard any nudge can drive before the network loses coherence. Can you find where that ceiling sits for your own control heads — and does it stay put as the material sets, or move with sigma: lower when the glass is molten and the network runs hot, higher when nearly cool? Where exactly does steering turn into clipping?

- AdaLN re-voices the whole body uniformly, but different blocks do different work. Is one global re-voicing always what you want — and what might you learn from a selective deafness, deliberately reducing a single block's sensitivity to that global hand, like one player told to hold steady against the swing of the rest?

- Sigma's walk from molten to set is not just *used* by the adaLN machinery but *assumed* by it, monotonic and single-valued. What happens to that assumption when you disturb the path — skewing the schedule, dropping in a re-melted seed, or imagining a per-moment molten-ness? What is the modulation quietly counting on that you would then be violating?

- A control head presses on the clay; the global vector would press on the hands. If you had both — a nudge on the latent *and* a new signal re-voicing the whole body — would they cooperate, or would re-voicing the hands change the very ceiling that bounds how hard the nudge can press?
