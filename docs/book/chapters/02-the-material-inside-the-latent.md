# Chapter 2 — The Material: Inside the Latent

Before the carver ever touches the block, someone has already transformed the tree. The trunk has been quartered, the grain read, the billet split off and seasoned. By the time it reaches the bench it is no longer wood-in-general; it is *this* prepared material, with its own ways of yielding and resisting. The carver works that material, not the forest it came from.

Stable Audio 3 does its own version of this in the autoencoder — SAME. It is the body of the instrument: it never generates anything, never decides what the music should be. It only translates. Its encoder takes the wild torrent of raw audio — forty-four thousand samples a second, two channels — and folds it into a thin, ordered stream of latent vectors; its decoder expands that stream back into audible sound. Compressor and expander, two halves of one trained translation.

The rate is worth deriving, because it tells you how much time gets folded into each slice. SAME takes a stride of about four thousand and ninety-six raw samples per slice, which over forty-four thousand-odd samples a second is roughly ten and three-quarter slices per second. So each slice answers for about ninety-three milliseconds — a span you can feel: a snare hit and its first bloom of decay, a plucked note's attack, half a syllable. Each is a column of about two hundred and fifty-six numbers, making the latent some four thousand times less verbose in time than the original. And here is what governs everything else in this book: the generative model never touches the raw wood — it has no machinery for raw audio at all. Everything the carver does happens inside this compressed stream. The latent is not a way-station; it is the only material there is. This is also why a single global molten-ness can govern the whole clip ahead (the sigma of Chapter 4): the material's rate and depth have already fixed the finest grain anything downstream can resolve.

## What a Column of Numbers Stands In For

So what *is* one of those slices? Tempting to imagine a tiny snapshot of the waveform — wiggle compressed into smaller wiggle — but that misleads. A slice is closer to a *description*: a compact account of what is sounding in that window, and how.

Think of how a luthier characterizes a struck bar. You do not hand someone the full motion of every fibre; you say: here are its first few resonant modes, here is how the energy rises and decays, here is the spectral weight, bright or dark — a handful of meaningful quantities standing in for an unfathomable amount of physical motion. A slice does the same for a moment of full stereo music: the dominant timbres, the rough envelope, the broad spectral balance, a sense of *what* is sounding, how loud, and roughly where in the stereo field.

But notice what that span cannot hold. A slice updates only about ten times a second — far slower than any pitch you can hear; a low bass note cycles forty or fifty times in the life of one slice, a cymbal thousands. So no slice can give the instantaneous *position* of a waveform; it cannot encode a sine wave as a sine wave, because the oscillation is far below the grid. What survives is the *summary* — that across this window the energy sat here, rose at this rate, spread this wide. This is the information floor: a whole tier of detail — the exact phase of a partial, the micro-jitter inside an attack — has no place to live here. A control that needs that tier is not hard to build; it is geometrically impossible *in this latent*, and would need a different mould.

So the compression is deeply lossy. The sample-by-sample waveform is gone for good; what survives is the *structure and feel* that carries musical identity — and that is not arbitrary. SAME did not discard detail by a fixed rule the way a crude downsampler would; it **learned** what to keep, set by its training objective. An autoencoder minimizing raw waveform error will smear a sharp transient if that lowers the average, while one trained against a perceptual or adversarial criterion guards the edges the ear weighs. So the loss *concentrates*, and you can often hear where: transient edges softened, a kick's snap rounded, a hi-hat gauzed — at the fast, sharp moments more than in the steady drone of a pad. This is the fidelity-budget a control head inherits: train it to steer a feature that lives where the autoencoder is blurriest, and it will feel imprecise no matter how well it was trained.

A subtler question rides along with stereo. The material is genuinely two-channel — the latent carries a spatial field — yet this book follows the rest of the instrument's controls and never returns to it, a deliberate omission these lines are its one due. A stereo image is not merely a left-right balance; much of the width and depth we hear lives in the *phase relationship* between the channels — the small timing and polarity differences that wrap a sound around the head. Treat that as the most fragile dimension in the material: broad placement survives compression well, but fine inter-channel phase coherence is exactly the fast, precise structure a slow summarizing grid is least equipped to hold. Before you build a control for stereo imagery, probe it by ear first — find whether the width you want to push on is even faithfully there.

## The Geometry Nobody Labelled

This is the part that matters most for a builder, because it is where your own hands will eventually go.

Those two hundred and fifty-six numbers in a slice are not a tidy instrument panel. None of them is "the brightness number." None is "the bass knob." They are simply the coordinates the encoder found most efficient for packing real music into a small space — a private shorthand the model invented for its own convenience, with no obligation to be legible to us.

And yet musical properties *are* in there — the decoder reconstructs brightness, density, attack, and spatial placement faithfully, so the information is present. The catch is that they do not line up with the individual numbers. Brightness is more likely a *direction* — a blend of many dimensions moving together, a diagonal through the space rather than one clean axis. Imagine a mixing console whose every fader simultaneously nudges level, a little EQ, a touch of pan, and some reverb send, in proportions nobody wrote on the strip; to raise "brightness" you would push a coordinated handful of them by just the right amounts. The control you want exists; it simply runs *oblique* to the controls the instrument exposes.

This has a sharp practical consequence. You cannot pick one coordinate, declare it "brightness," and push; there is no such coordinate. Even a careful attempt — fit a straight-line reading from the latent to a brightness number, then steer along it — is already a search for a *direction*, and the direction it finds may not be real. It can be an artifact of the sounds you fitted it on, dissolving the moment you try a new kind of source. This is why the unlabelled geometry of this space makes a whole later family of tools necessary: the control head of Chapter 13 exists because there is no signpost to read directly, only a direction to be triangulated from examples.

And those directions differ in their grain. A direction clean and consistent across many sounds is a smooth edge to carve. A tangled one — pointing one way for drums and another for strings — is a knot, and the knot tells you something true: the latent has *conflated* two distinct phenomena under one apparent feature. Whatever "brightness" you fitted is really two things wearing one name. You feel that knot the moment you push, and no training strength files it smooth. The grain was in the wood.

## The Decoder as a Place to Put Your Hands

The decoder is its own intervention site, distinct from the prompt, the carver, and the control heads — and genuinely underused.

The carver settles a latent; the decoder — SAME's second half — translates it back into audible air. That translation is not a neutral pane of glass. It is a *learned voicing*: a fixed mapping from latent-geometry to sound, with its own character — the body and varnish that colour every note a soundboard speaks, no matter what the strings do.

Suppose the cheapest way to satisfy the decoder's training objective tilted it a hair toward extra high-frequency energy. You would not find this as a knob; you would hear it as a *consistent house sound* — every output, regardless of prompt or seed or control head, arriving a touch more present than the latent asked for, a sheen you cannot prompt away because it is added after the music is decided. A musician detects this as a luthier detects a flattering varnish: by running many different "strings" through the same body and noticing the colour they all share.

The decoder does more than colour, and the *more* constrains what you can steer. Because the latent comes in discrete slices, the decoder must make them flow without a seam at every boundary — it stitches them, by overlap and learned windowing, into continuous air. That has a cost: a feature you change within one slice cannot snap on instantaneously at its edge, because the decoder is smoothing across that edge to keep the seam from clicking. The same continuity that saves you from artifacts blunts the sharpest, most local steering — a balance the decoder has already chosen.

And this voicing is, in principle, separable from everything upstream. So imagine adjusting *only the decoder* — the carver frozen, every control head still on its targets — but changing how a whole class of latents *sounds* on the way out: a different finish over the same carving. If you could, what class would you make speak differently — and, the harder half, what could you *not* fix this way, because it was already lost before the decoder saw it? A re-voicing at the mouth cannot restore detail the encoder discarded; it can only recolour what arrives.

## The Grid Is a Choice, Not a Law

One last property is easy to mistake for physics: its resolution. About ten and three-quarter slices a second; about two hundred and fifty-six numbers a slice. These feel like constants of nature. They are dimensions of the mould, chosen when SAME was built — a training-time decision, the body-change the adapter and finetune chapters take up directly. Knowing the latent's *composition* must come first: you cannot decide where to hook a correction into the encoder or decoder until you know what the material is made to keep.

Make the grid finer in time and each slice carries a shorter window. You gain temporal precision — the ability to pin a fast transient — but pay with a longer stream to carve and every downstream tool working at the new rate. Make it coarser and a leaner, faster substrate smears fine timing. And granularity does more than set precision; it gates the carving *order*. A carver works coarse-to-fine, roughing out the largest structures first — and a coarse-in-time grid can only rough out coarse-in-time structure, deciding which musical structures are even *available* to shape early and which can only ever be implied.

Likewise the depth. Widen each slice past two hundred and fifty-six and a moment holds more nuance, more separable strands, at the cost of a larger space to learn and navigate; narrow it and the material gets terser. None is right or wrong; each is a different *wood*. What would you trade a coarser-in-time, deeper-per-slice latent *for* — and which controls would that make possible, and which foreclose? You cannot ask a control to steer a temporal detail finer than the grid can represent; the material has no place to put it. The resolution of the clay sets the ceiling on the precision of every tool that ever touches it.

That ceiling is the lever to carry forward. The first thing to know about any control you imagine is whether the material can even hold the detail it would need to grip. Before you reach for the carver, you have already inherited the wood.

## Openings

- The encoder decided what survives compression, and a *differently trained* encoder would decide differently. If one could keep the micro-timing or the inter-channel phase this one smears away, would that be the same instrument with new clay, or a genuinely different instrument — and which losses in the present material are the ones you would most want a new mould to repair?

- The meaningful directions in the latent are *found, not planned*, and some are smooth while others are knotted and source-dependent. Is there a way to feel a direction's grain *before* you commit to building a control around it — to learn in advance whether you are carving with the wood or against it, rather than discovering the knot only when you push?

- The decoder re-voices latent into sound at the very mouth of the instrument, and stitches the slices into continuous air. Where, between that stitching and that colouring, is there room for a builder to put a hand — and what kind of control could live in the decoder alone that could live nowhere else upstream?
