# Chapter 9 - The Dials Already On the Instrument: Guidance and Re-Melting

Before you train anything — before you bolt on a single tuning peg, before you bond a laminate to the soundboard — go back to the instrument as it shipped and notice the two dials fitted at the factory. They sit on the front panel of every generation. They cost nothing to turn, leave no permanent mark, and a player who never opens the case can still get an enormous range of voices out of them. One reaches into the *sampling process* — the carve itself — and changes how hard the prompt presses. The other reaches into the *initialisation* — what the carve starts from — and changes how much of a real sound you let dissolve before carving begins.

They are the *coarse* dials, and saying so plainly is the reason this book does not stop here. CFG offers "push harder." SDEdit offers "melt deeper." Both are blunt: they act on the whole output at once, with a single number. The chapters ahead refine that bluntness toward *precision* — targeting one feature instead of all (the control heads), applying force only at a chosen moment in the carve (sigma-banded guidance), sculpting one region instead of the whole clip. And holding two dials of such different principle side by side teaches something about control that neither teaches alone.

## The First Dial: Classifier-Free Guidance

Here is a thing the instrument does that you would never guess from the outside. Every time it generates with a prompt, it runs the carving process *twice*, in parallel, at every step. Once with your words present and steering; once completely blind, as if no prompt had ever been spoken, generating from nothing but its own untethered sense of what music is.

Why do the work twice? Because the difference between those two runs is precisely, and only, the prompt's *pull*. The blind run shows where the material drifts on its own; the prompted run shows where the words want it to go. Subtract one from the other and what remains is the pure vector of the prompt's influence — the actual authority of language over this moment of sound. The blind run is a control surface in the literal sense: a reference against which the prompt's effect is measured.

And it must be *computed*, fresh, each time — you cannot pre-store it. The model's untutored drift depends on where the material is at that step, which depends on the seed and on everything the prompt has already nudged into place. The blind tendency is *context-specific*: change the seed, the prompt, or how far along the carve you are, and "where it would go on its own" changes too. So both runs ride together, the blind one perpetually re-answering "and where would you drift from *here*, with no words?"

Classifier-free guidance — CFG — is the dial that decides how loudly you amplify that gap. At a guidance of one, you take the prompted run and go. Turn it up and you exaggerate the difference, pushing the material *further* in the prompted direction than the model left to itself would have — past the prompted prediction, leaning hard into the words. Turn it down toward zero and the prompt's pull goes slack; the carving follows the blind run more and more, letting the model's own untutored instinct fill the space between your words.

Recall from Chapter 5 that the prompt does not touch the carve everywhere — it enters through one specific door, the cross-attention, where words reach in and steer the sound. CFG is the dial on *that* door. It does not add new words or change their meaning; it changes how hard whatever comes through is allowed to press. Raise the guidance and you are not making the prompt louder so much as making its *consequence* louder — amplifying the difference the cross-attention door already made.

The temptation is to treat this as a quality knob — more guidance, more obedience, therefore better. Resist that: *neither extreme is simply better*, and the relationship is not monotonic. High guidance sharpens the focus on your description, but press too hard and you over-correct — too literal, too saturated, every adjective at full strength at once, the audio equivalent of a mix where every track is slammed to the ceiling and nothing breathes. Edges harden, transients spike, the thing announces its own prompt at you. Low guidance lets the music breathe — but relax too far and it wanders, drifting toward whatever the blind run happened to want.

There is a clean way to picture *why* low guidance breathes and high does not. High guidance follows the prompted prediction tightly, so the spread of outputs across different seeds collapses inward; low guidance leans on the freer blind run, so the spread widens. The variety you lose at high CFG is not noise being cleaned up — it is genuine alternatives being squeezed out.

So the sweet spot is not a number you look up — CFG is a *voicing* act, not a setting. It moves with the prompt: a dense, over-specified prompt may already be straining and wants a *gentler* hand; a sparse one may need a firmer hand to register at all. And it moves with the *instrument*, because it is calibrated against the *scale* of the latent being pushed. SA3's larger model carries a latent of different magnitude than its smaller siblings; a value that whispers on one can shout on another, so cookbook numbers borrowed from another model will mislead you. (Chapter 13 takes up *calibrating force to the latent's scale* properly; for now, simply distrust borrowed settings.) A bell-founder tuning a finished casting does not consult a chart that says "grind 0.4 millimetres from the inner rim" — they strike it, hear where the partials sit, take a little metal off, strike again. Finding the right guidance is exactly that loop, calibrated by ear, against this prompt, on this instrument.

And here the dial stops being a single thing. Recall from Chapter 6 that sigma is already re-voicing every block of the network through the adaLN modulation — global knobs set by how molten the material is. CFG re-voices it too, but from a different direction: not by *where in the walk* you are but by *how hard the prompt presses*. Whether those two re-voicings cooperate or fight is a real open question, and it bites because the prompt's pull is not constant across the walk. From Chapter 3 you know the carve proceeds coarse-to-fine: the molten beginning decides large structure — form, tempo, the skeleton — while the set end leaves only surface free to move. A hard push at the molten beginning therefore shapes *structure*; the same push near the set end shapes only *texture*.

You can exploit that asymmetry deliberately. Suppose you want a generation locked to a tempo but with the timbre left loose and alive. You would want a *firm* hand early, while structure is decided at high sigma, and a *lighter* hand late, while texture settles — high guidance where the beat is laid down, low where the grain is filled in. The instrument ships with one number for the whole carve, so you cannot do this with the factory dial alone. But knowing the dial *means different things at different molten-nesses* is exactly the intuition the later sigma-banded controls are built to act on.

## The Second Dial: Re-Melting a Real Sound

The second dial reaches into a different part of the instrument entirely — not the carving but the block you start carving from.

Ordinarily, generation begins from pure noise: a latent of maximum molten-ness, sigma at one, every possibility open. The Chapter 3 walk proceeds from there. Even that bare starting draw is already a quiet control surface — *which* random block you pour in (the seed number) fixes which performance you get, the cheapest dial of all, costing nothing and changing everything. But no law says the block must start formless. You can instead begin from a *real* piece of audio — encode it into the latent material the instrument speaks (Chapter 2), partly re-melt it, and carve from *that*.

This is SDEdit — audio-to-audio re-synthesis. The crucial thing is that the seed sound is *not* a prompt. It does not enter through the cross-attention door where words come in; it enters as a *physical initialisation*, the literal starting material of the carve. So when you seed *and* prompt at once, you are not pitting one against the other — you have two concurrent steering channels on the same latent. **The seed determines where the material begins; the prompt determines which direction the carving leans. Both act on the same latent simultaneously.** Not the prompt overwriting the seed, not the seed waiting to be refined by it — two voices pushing the same carve at once.

How far you re-melt the block is the second control, layered over the first. "Re-melting" is a sigma choice, nothing more — you already own its meaning from Chapter 4. You pick a point on the molten-ness walk, drop your real sound in, and let the carve run to set. The depth of that drop is everything.

Melt *shallow* — near the set end, at low sigma — and you barely disturb it. The fine surface survives almost intact; the carve only re-settles the outermost skin. This is the zone for *touch-up*: cleaning a noisy recording, nudging a timbre, re-skinning a sound while its rhythm, phrasing, and identity stay plainly intact. You return nearly the same thing, lightly reconsidered.

Melt *deep* — near the molten beginning, at high sigma — and you offer the seed's very bones up for reconsideration. Only the coarsest structure survives; the model rebuilds everything finer from scratch, guided by the prompt and its own sense of music. This is the zone for *gesture theft*: keep the broad arc, the rough rhythmic feel, the sense of motion, and let the instrument rebuild the actual voice from nothing. You brought a sketch; you return a different performance that merely *moves* like it did. The middle band keeps the seed's larger phrasing while its surface is up for grabs — recognisably related to the source, audibly a new thing.

A deep melt has a quieter, diagnostic use too. Re-melt almost to the bone, listen to what comes back changed versus unchanged, and you are asking: *what does the latent preserve about this sound when everything fine is dissolved?* Whatever survives is what the encoder's compression (Chapter 2) and the diffusion's restructuring together consider *essential* — a probe into the instrument's own sense of what a sound fundamentally *is*.

So SDEdit is two controls in one: what you pour in (the seed) and how much you let dissolve (the melt-depth). They are independent — the same seed melted shallow and deep gives two different results; one melt-depth on two seeds gives two more. The space is a grid, not a line, and mostly unexplored — but not uniformly inviting. The *safe* corner is shallow melt with a simple, clean seed: a light touch-up, low risk, predictable. The *experimental* corner is deep melt with a complex, busy seed: you ask the instrument to rebuild a great deal while holding a tangled gesture, and it may hold the wrong thing or lose coherence. Deep melts of thin or ambiguous seeds tend to fail outright — too little structure to anchor the rebuild. Start in the safe corner and scan toward the experimental one one axis at a time, so that when something interesting happens you know which dial did it.

Do not confuse SDEdit with masking (Chapter 10): SDEdit re-melts the *entire* seed uniformly — every slice gets the same molten-ness, because sigma is still a single global number (Chapter 4) — whereas masking freezes part of the seed entirely and regenerates the rest from noise. One is a dial of *depth* applied evenly; the other is a decision of *where*, applied slice by slice. Chapter 10 owns that second tool; here it is enough to keep the two from blurring.

## Two Dials, One Boundary

Notice how unlike each other these two dials are, and let the contrast do its teaching.

CFG lives in the *sampling process*. It changes how the carve behaves — how hard the prompt presses — and needs no input but words and a number. It is holistic. SDEdit lives in the *initialisation*. It changes what the carve starts from, needs a seed sound and a melt-depth, and is anchoring: it ties the output to a real piece of audio you brought. One is a pressure; the other is a starting point.

Yet for all that difference they share an address. *The Map* (Chapter 14) will eventually lay out a whole set of axes for placing any control — where it intervenes, when, what it costs, how far it reaches, and more. On every one of those axes that separates these two factory dials from the heavier machinery *later* in the book, they agree: both act at *inference time*, both cost nothing beyond the run, both are perfectly reversible — turn the dial back and the instrument is exactly as it was. The single axis worth naming here is the one this chapter is built to teach.

And both are *open-loop* — the deepest thing they share, and the line the rest of the book is written across. You set the guidance, set the melt-depth, let the carve run, and nothing measures the result and corrects it mid-flight. No tuner in the loop. That is not a limitation of "inference-time" controls in general: the control heads of Chapter 13 also run at inference time and reverse cleanly — yet they *close the loop*, measuring a musical quantity as the carve proceeds and correcting toward it. The real divide is not training-time versus inference-time but *open-loop versus closed-loop* — set-and-forget versus measure-and-correct — and these two factory dials sit firmly on the open-loop side. Chapter 12 returns to this same boundary to ask what kind of teaching each side calls for.

That is the point of holding them together. They mark the *outer wall* of free, per-performance, open-loop control: inside it you can obey or breathe, start from noise or from a remembered sound, preserve much or regenerate most — all without altering a single weight or ever listening back mid-carve. The chapters that follow step outside the wall; some open the body and ask for data, compute, and permanence, some bolt on a new sense and close the loop. Knowing where this boundary lies is what lets you judge, honestly, whether you need to cross it at all.

## Openings

- The blind, unprompted run is a real generation in its own right. Does it produce something coherent and musical, or closer to static? Whatever it produces tells you how much this instrument knows about music *without any words at all* — and it matters practically, because the same blind run is what a low-guidance generation or a prompt-free SDEdit leans on. If it makes music, you can trust the seed to carry a re-melt alone; if it makes mush, the prompt is doing more work than it looks. Go listen to it directly.

- Seed and melt-depth are two separate controls. Hold one fixed and scan the other — one recording melted shallow to deep, or one melt-depth across a row of seeds. What does that grid sound like as a *space*, and where do the interesting failures live, between the safe and experimental corners?

- When you re-melt deeper, what dissolves *first* — rhythm, timbre, pitch, sense of space? (frontier) A consistent order would tell you something about the geometry of the latent itself: which musical properties live in the coarse structure that survives a deep melt, and which live only in the fine surface a shallow melt preserves.

- CFG and SDEdit turn at the same time. Do they compound, interfere, or ignore each other? (speculative) Can you reason about it from first principles — a deep melt wants the prompt to rebuild what was dissolved and may want firm guidance, a shallow melt wants it slack — or are you left auditioning combinations until your ear finds the ones that work?

- If a hard push means *structure* at the molten beginning and *texture* at the set end, what would you want the guidance to *do* across a single carve — rise, fall, hold, or move in some shape keyed to the music? The factory gives one number for the whole walk. Once you can imagine the curve you wish you could draw, you have already half-specified a control that does not yet ship — exactly the kind of wish the rest of this book is here to help you say out loud.
