# Chapter 7 - A Sense of Time and a Scratch of Memory

An experienced musician knows their position in a piece not by consulting a clock but by feeling how far they are from things. Not "I am at measure thirty-two" but "I am six beats after the last downbeat, twelve after the chord change, and the turnaround is coming up fast." This is proprioception — the sense by which a body knows where its own limbs are without looking. A drummer who drops into a fill and lands the one without counting has it. A string quartet that breathes together through a ritardando has it. It is a sense of *relative distance*, felt rather than measured, and it is what keeps an improviser coherent across spans of time too long to hold in working memory all at once.

SA3 has something quietly analogous, and this chapter is about understanding it well enough to treat it as a lever. We are leaving the large mechanisms behind for a moment — the carver, the attention, the voicing of the whole body — and turning to two small parts. Small does not mean unimportant. A well-placed sound-post is a stick of spruce you could snap between two fingers, and it governs the entire voice of a violin. Small parts at the right seam are exactly where a builder's hands can reach in and change everything downstream.

## How the model feels where it is

The part that gives the model its sense of time is called rotary positional embedding, or RoPE. The single clearest way to understand it is to set it beside the alternative it replaces, once, and then move on.

You *could* give each slice of the latent stream an absolute address — slice one, slice two, slice three, up to the end — the way you number the bars in a written score. RoPE refuses to do that. Instead of stamping each slice with where it *is*, it encodes how far apart any two slices *are*. Absolute addressing ties a lesson to a location; relative distance frees it. That is the whole comparison, and it is enough to carry the chapter.

Here is why the difference is not academic. A model trained on absolute addresses learns phrase structure *at those addresses* — bars one through eight, say — and when the same phrase shape recurs later, at a different set of addresses, it has to learn the lesson again from scratch. The audible failure of that is concrete: the same melodic contour can come out subtly *different* at bar eight than at bar one, because to the model those are simply different places, with no guarantee they behave alike. It is the helplessness of a guitarist who has only ever practised a scale from the third fret and freezes the moment you ask for it on the eighth. RoPE removes that brittleness by construction.

How it does so connects directly back to "How Moments Listen" (Chapter 5), where the model's faculty for letting every moment listen to every other was laid out in full. RoPE does not start those conversations and does not decide what is said in them; it only colours the *weight* attention assigns, according to the distance between the two moments talking. The two are a single working pair: attention proposes which moments talk; RoPE inflects how much their separation-in-time should pull them together or hold them apart. So the model knows "these two are close" or "these two are far" without ever knowing what bar number either falls on. RoPE is the proprioceptive nerve; attention is the muscle. A nerve, severed or rerouted, changes everything the muscle does — but it never moves the limb itself.

The consequence is portability, and portability is both the feature and the cost. Because the model's knowledge rides on *relative* distances rather than fixed addresses, a structural lesson learned anywhere transfers everywhere: the feel of a downbeat four beats after the last is the same feel near the opening or deep in the coda. That is precisely the position-independence a trained ear has. But the same property that makes the ear portable also means the ear was only ever schooled on the *range* of distances it met in training. Portability is guaranteed within that range and merely hoped-for beyond it — a distinction the second half of this chapter turns on.

One clarification a musician will reach for, because they will assume it: does this relative-distance sense govern *harmony* — voice-leading, tension and resolution — or only rhythm and large form? We do not have a clean separation to offer. RoPE conditions every moment-to-moment conversation indiscriminately; it does not know harmony from rhythm. Whatever the model learned about how a suspension wants to resolve, or how a dominant leans toward its tonic, it learned *through* these distance-coloured conversations, because that is the only sense of time it has. So relative distance almost certainly touches harmonic movement too — but as a substrate the model built its harmony *on*, not a knob you will find labelled "resolution." Bear that in mind before you bend distances expecting the rhythm to warp while the harmony stays put. It may not divide so neatly.

## A small notepad carried alongside

Beside this sense of time, the model carries a second small thing: a handful of learned memory tokens. These are extra slots that travel through every attention layer right next to the audio material, but they are not tied to any particular moment in the piece. They do not represent "the sound at second four." They float free of the timeline.

Before we say what they might be *for*, let us be honest about what we do not know, all at once and up front: we cannot read these tokens in any language we understand, their contents are the model's own private shorthand, and we do not yet know what it costs — in coherence, in stability — to write to them by hand. Hold those unknowns together. They are the price of admission to thinking about this seam at all.

With that said, think of the tokens as a very small notepad the model keeps at the edge of the workbench. As it refines its understanding of the piece, step by step, it can read from and write to this notepad — jotting down global impressions that belong to no single slice. Make it concrete: imagine a long crescendo. The fact that *energy is accumulating across the whole passage* has nowhere natural to live in the moment-by-moment material — each slice only knows its own loudness, not the trend. A free-floating slot is exactly where a running sense of "the build is two-thirds done, keep climbing" could be held and carried forward. In studio terms, the tokens are less like a track on the tape and more like the engineer's margin notes — a place where observations about the whole take accumulate without crowding the music itself.

The *existence* of such a channel is what should quicken a builder's pulse. It is a wire that already runs the full length of the instrument and touches every layer, carrying a signal that is neither audio nor prompt. This is the same proof-of-concept the duration conditioner offers in Chapter 8: a single untethered number — intended length in seconds — threaded into the right place, steering the whole generation. The memory tokens are a second, independent witness that an untethered signal *can* touch everything. A builder hunting for places to inject a new kind of information has, in these two examples, evidence that the instrument is already wired for it.

## Two confirmed seams

So we have two modest mechanisms, and each is a genuine seam — a place where the instrument is joined, and therefore a place a builder might pry.

The first seam is the relative-time sense. If the model feels time as distance, the distances are in principle something you could manipulate — and here a load-bearing distinction enters, one Chapter 10 leans on entirely. There are two very different things a builder might mean by "manipulating RoPE." One is to change *which positions the model sees* — to feed it a window of the timeline, or to tell it a fresh slice sits at position N rather than zero, without touching the machinery that turns position into distance. The other is to reach into that machinery itself and *rescale what distance means* — to make far-apart moments read as neighbours, or the reverse. Sliding-window long-form rendering, the subject of Chapter 10, lives almost entirely in the *first* kind: managing positions, not rewriting the distance function. Keep the two apart; they cost different things and break different ways.

Suppose you took the harder, second path and compressed the distances the model perceives — packed a long passage so its moments read as close together, the way a film editor speeds a sequence by cutting frames. Or expanded them, stretching a short passage so nearby moments read as far apart. Here is a starting hypothesis to listen *against*, so you are not auditioning blind: compressing distances will likely degrade long-form coherence — the model, told everything is near, may lose its grip on large structure and let the piece wander or repeat. Expanding distances may do the opposite damage — fine detail and local groove fragmenting as the model treats true neighbours as strangers. Those are guesses, not findings. But they tell you what to *listen for*: large-form drift under compression, micro-fragmentation under expansion. The honest builder treats this as a question to be auditioned, not a result to assume.

There is a cheaper test than weeks of listening, and it makes the frontier auditable. Self-attention, at each layer, leaves a record of which moment-pairs it weighted heavily — Chapter 5's conversation, made visible. RoPE imposes its own distance-weighting on top. Lay one over the other: where does attention actually concentrate, and does its concentration track the distances RoPE is feeding it, or ignore them? Nudge a distance — if the attention pattern shifts in step, the model is genuinely *leaning* on relative time and your lever has purchase. If nothing downstream moves, you have learned — for the cost of a single render, not a season — that this seam is, for this material, more decorative than load-bearing. That turns "is RoPE a real handle?" from a faith into a measurement.

This seam, then, connects forward to Chapter 10's long-form and looping work and to the frontier of *per-moment* time, where the handling of position becomes load-bearing in a new way.

The second seam is the notepad. The memory tokens are filled by the model for the model's own purposes — but what if, at inference time, you could *seed* them with a signal of your choosing, or *clamp* them to a steady value throughout generation? You would be writing in the margin of the engineer's notebook in your own hand and seeing whether the band downstream reads it. Note the shape of this move carefully, because Chapter 12 will sharpen it: seeding-and-clamping is *open-loop*. You write once, you do not look back, you do not correct based on what the music does. It is set-and-forget, like choosing a prompt — the opposite of the measure-and-correct feedback that Chapter 12 builds its control heads around. Naming it open-loop now is what lets that later contrast land.

What would you write there — a sense of intended density, a marker of "this is the climax," a value some control head computed? And, crucially, how would you *read back* whether your scribble had any effect at all? The diagnostic instinct from the first seam applies here too: change what you wrote, hold everything else fixed, and listen for — or measure — whether anything downstream actually moved. A margin the model never learned to consult will swallow your handwriting without a trace, and a number that drifts on its own will fool you into thinking you steered when you only watched.

That is the honest heart of this chapter. These are small parts at real seams, tantalising precisely because they look accessible — but "looks like a seam" and "is a usable handle" are different claims. The memory tokens might be a rich channel or a vestigial one the model barely leans on. The relative-time sense might bend gracefully or shatter the moment you push it past the distances it met in training. And there is a quieter worry that spans both seams and reaches back into "Voicing the Whole Body" (Chapter 6): the body-wide re-voicing that chapter describes runs the full melt sigma governs (Chapter 4's one dial). RoPE's distance-sense is supposed to stay *stable* across that whole melt — two moments are no farther apart just because the material got more set. Do those two levers stay politely independent, or does pulling on RoPE while the body is being re-voiced produce coupling neither of us can predict — a wolf-tone where two well-behaved adjustments meet? We do not know. It is exactly the kind of interaction a builder must audition for, not assume away.

## Openings

- If the model feels time as relative distance rather than absolute address, what does a "distance" actually structure — only rhythm and large form, or harmonic movement and resolution too? *(frontier)* Settle this in your own ear first, because every question below depends on what you believe lives in relative time.

- Once you know what distance structures: what happens when you present the model with positional relationships it never saw — a very long repeating cycle, a metre from outside its training? *(speculative)* Does its portable ear stretch to reach them, or is "portable" only true within the range of distances it has met?

- *Then,* and only then, the manipulations: if you compressed or expanded the perceived distances, what do you *expect* to hear — large-form drift, fine-detail fragmentation, something else — and what would that expectation reveal about which musical structures you believe live in relative time? *(speculative)*

- The memory tokens are a notepad in the model's own shorthand. If you seeded or clamped them, what would you write — and, harder, how would you design the read-back test that tells you the model actually consulted it, rather than fooling yourself with a number that moved on its own?

- A free-floating channel that touches every layer and carries something other than audio or words fits the memory tokens — but it is also a *specification* for a control seam in the abstract. Where else in the instrument might such a channel already exist, unnoticed, waiting to be repurposed?

- These are the smallest parts the book examines closely, and the most frontier-flavoured. What makes a small mechanism worth prying open — and how would you decide, before sinking weeks into it, whether a seam is a true handle or only the appearance of one?
