# Preface

Bart Hopkin's *Experimental Musical Instruments* — and the zine of the same name before it — has guided more builders than its modest size suggests. It does not tell you how to make instruments; it tells you how instruments *work*: why a thicker bar rings lower, why a closed tube speaks an octave below an open one, why one resonator couples richly to a vibrating tine and another swallows it. Hopkin's wager was that a builder armed with intuition invents things no recipe could anticipate, while one armed only with recipes builds exactly what the recipe describes and nothing more. This book is written in that spirit.

So: a book of questions, not blueprints. No parameter table to copy, no settings file to paste, no step-by-step procedure to a guaranteed result. Instead, an attempt to build your intuition about one instrument — Stable Audio 3 — thoroughly enough that you can formulate your own questions and go discover the answers. Most sections end by *opening* a question rather than closing one: the closed answers will be obsolete before the ink dries; the open questions are yours to keep.

**Who this is for.** A musician and a maker. You build or repair instruments, or you have spent enough time with acoustics that resonance, damping, envelopes, filters, and the behaviour of vibrating bodies are not abstractions but things you have felt under your hands. You know the basics of digital sound — spectra, sampling, modulation — well enough to follow along. And you have begun to steer a machine that generates audio: training small control heads, tuning guidance, bending the output toward what you hear in your head.

**Who this is not for.** You need not be a software engineer, a mathematician, or a machine-learning researcher. There is no code here, no loss functions as equations, no matrices, no derivations. Where a mechanism must be described, it is described as a *role* — what it does and where it sits — in the language of bodies and bridges, gathers of molten glass, bracing and wolf-tones, the mixing console and the restorer's mask. If you have ever voiced a soundboard or chosen where to spend your effort on a repair, you already think in the right terms.

**The central image.** Treat Stable Audio 3 as an instrument you can open and put your hands inside. From the outside it is a text box and a button — but that is the casing. Inside is a three-part construction as interdependent as the body, soundboard, and bridge of a fine guitar: a compressor-expander that holds the *material* sound is made from, a carver that settles formless noise into music, and a translator that turns your words into something the carver can feel. We will name the real parts — the autoencoder, the diffusion transformer, attention, the timestep, classifier-free guidance, the control heads — but always as functions and handholds, never as code.

**The one throughline.** Every part of this instrument, and every joint between parts, is a place where control can be *injected*. That question — *where, and how, can a builder put their hands in to add or change control* — runs through every chapter. We ask it two ways each time: what you can do at **training time**, when you reshape the body of the instrument itself, and what you can do at **inference time**, fresh at each performance, leaving the body untouched. Holding those apart, and feeling which a given problem calls for, is most of the craft.

**How to read this.** Read it in order the first time; the chapters build a shared vocabulary, and the later ones lean hard on the earlier. After that, wander. When a section marks something *known*, *speculative*, or *frontier*, believe the label — telling those apart is itself a skill the book means to teach. And when an open question at the foot of a section catches you, stop reading and go to the instrument. This book is at its best as a thing you put down.

The deepest service these pages can offer is not a technique but a grammar — enough of the right words that, when you finally know what control you want, you can say it precisely enough to be answered. The instrument's capacity stays locked until someone speaks to it correctly. Let us teach you to speak.
