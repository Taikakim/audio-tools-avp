# Preface

There is a book by Bart Hopkin called *Experimental Musical Instruments* — and a zine before it of the same name — that has guided more builders than its modest size suggests. It does not, for the most part, tell you how to make instruments. It tells you how instruments *work*: why a thicker bar rings lower, why a closed tube speaks an octave below an open one, why one resonator couples richly to a vibrating tine and another swallows it. Hopkin's wager was that a builder armed with intuition will invent things no recipe could anticipate, while a builder armed only with recipes will build exactly what the recipe describes and not one thing more. This book is written in that spirit, and owes it a debt I want to name plainly at the outset.

So: this is a book of questions, not blueprints. You will not find a parameter table to copy, a settings file to paste, or a step-by-step procedure to follow to a guaranteed result. You will find, instead, an attempt to build your intuition about a particular instrument — Stable Audio 3 — thoroughly enough that you can formulate your own questions and go discover the answers yourself. Most sections end by *opening* a question rather than closing one. That is on purpose. The closed answers will be obsolete before the ink dries; the open questions are yours to keep.

**Who this is for.** You are a musician and a maker. You build or repair instruments, or you have spent enough time with acoustics that resonance, damping, envelopes, filters, and the behaviour of vibrating bodies are not abstractions to you but things you have felt under your hands. You know the basics of digital sound — spectra, sampling, modulation — well enough to follow along. And you have begun, recently, to experiment with steering a machine that generates audio: training small control heads, tuning guidance, trying to bend the output toward what you hear in your head.

**Who this is not for.** You are not required to be a software engineer, a mathematician, or a machine-learning researcher, and this book does not address them. There is no code in these pages. There are no loss functions written as equations, no matrices, no derivations. Where a mechanism must be described, it is described as a *role* — what it does and where it sits — in the language of bodies and bridges, gathers of molten glass, bracing and wolf-tones, the mixing console and the restorer's mask. If you have ever voiced a soundboard or chosen where to spend your effort on a repair, you already think in the right terms.

**The central image.** Treat Stable Audio 3 as an instrument you can open and put your hands inside. From the outside it looks like a text box and a button — but that is the casing. Inside is a three-part construction as interdependent as the body, soundboard, and bridge of a fine guitar: a compressor-expander that holds the *material* sound is made from, a carver that settles formless noise into music, and a translator that turns your words into something the carver can feel. We will name the real parts — the autoencoder, the diffusion transformer, attention, the timestep, classifier-free guidance, the control heads — but always as functions and handholds, never as code.

**The one throughline.** Every part of this instrument, and every joint between parts, is a place where control can be *injected*. That single question — *where, and how, can a builder put their hands in to change or add control* — runs through every chapter. We will ask it two ways each time: what can you do at **training time**, when you reshape the body of the instrument itself, and what can you do at **inference time**, fresh at each performance, leaving the body untouched. Holding those two apart, and learning to feel which a given problem calls for, is most of the craft.

**How to read this.** Read it in order the first time; the chapters build a shared vocabulary, and the later ones lean hard on the earlier. After that, wander. When a section marks something as *known*, *speculative*, or *frontier*, believe the label — telling those apart is itself a skill the book means to teach. And when an open question at the foot of a section catches you, stop reading and go to the instrument. This book is at its best as a thing you put down.

The deepest service these pages can offer is not a technique but a grammar — enough of the right words that, when you finally know what control you want, you can say it precisely enough to be answered. The instrument's capacity stays locked until someone speaks to it correctly. Let us teach you to speak.
